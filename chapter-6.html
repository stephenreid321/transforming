{elena’s story}

The conference is being held in the main hall of the municipal building. The grand one on the first floor which is normally used for formal ceremonies. Two hundred people. Managers, employees, elected officials, and a few curious people from other municipalities who have heard something is happening in this department.

Elena appears on the programme twice: once as a panelist in the morning session about new ways to organise public sector work, and once in the afternoon, giving a short presentation on her team’s journey. She has given versions of this talk seven times over the last six months: three times internally, twice at a national conference, twice to a delegation from a neighbouring municipality that had reached out after someone forwarded them the internal newsletter article about their success. Each time she gives the talk, she finds she has to update it, because things keep changing quickly in her department.

Her team’s work is described in the third paragraph of the introduction: “A pilot in shared leadership that has produced measurable improvements in both team engagement and service outcomes, including a 23% reduction in staff sick leave, improved response times despite a flat budget, and qualitative feedback from care recipients indicating significantly increased sense of connection and support.”

She reads the 23% figure and reflects back on February, one year ago, when she was staring at a kpi dashboard that told her almost nothing about Mrs. Andersson. She had not set out to improve the kpis. She had set out to create conditions where her team could do the work they actually believed in. The kpis had followed quietly, like a byproduct.

She thinks this is probably the most important thing she has learned and also the hardest to put on a presentation slide.

The morning panel goes well. Elena speaks less than the other two panelists (a habit she has been cultivating) and asks the audience a question at the end rather than summarising her points. A few people appear slightly startled by this. Most lean forward.

In the break afterwards, she is approached by several people. She has learned to be present in these conversations, to resist the urge to hand people a reading list and move on. She asks questions. She listens to what people are actually asking beneath what they say they’re asking, which is usually not “what did you do?” but “do you think this could work for someone like me?”

Jonas finds her between sessions. He looks, she thinks, like someone who belongs in rooms like this one. He had facilitated this morning’s opening activity: a 20-minute exercise with the whole room that he had designed himself, drawing on the embodied practices from Karin’s workshops, but adapting them for a group of 200 skeptical public servants. It had worked out better than anyone expected. She had watched from the side of the room, genuinely surprised, as 200 people who’d been checking their phones when they arrived were suddenly in conversation with someone they’d never met, talking about something real.

“How did it feel?” she asks him. “Terrifying,” he says. “Good.”

She nods. She knows exactly how this combination feels. He tells her that two people have already approached him about the community coffee morning model: one from social services, one from the youth department. He has their cards. He is going to follow up next week. He says this without asking her whether he should, without checking whether it fits inside some boundary she has drawn. He says it the way someone says a thing they have already decided.

She remembers the person who’d turned his phone over and over in his hands at the Monday morning meeting. The one who wrote the date at the top of his notebook and nothing else for 20 minutes.

The afternoon session ends, and the formal programme dissolves into the noise of 200 people deciding whether to go home or keep talking. Elena is gathering her things when a man she doesn’t recognise stops beside her.

He introduces himself as David. He manages a team in the urban infrastructure department with 12 people, mostly engineers, and a culture he describes, with a kind of exhausted honesty, as “very hierarchical, very technical, very convinced that this kind of thing isn’t for them.” He’d been at the morning panel. He’d heard her talk in the afternoon. He looks like a man who has been sitting with a question for a long time and has finally found someone he thinks might have the answer.

“I want to do what you’ve done,” he says. “I just don’t know where to start. What’s the process? What did you do first?”

Elena’s first instinct is precise and immediate: she knows exactly what to give him. The personal canvas exercise. The open hour meeting format. Starting with culture before structure. Having honest conversations first. She could map it out on a napkin in 10 minutes. She has basically done exactly that, four times over the last six months, for four different people who’d asked her similar questions.

She opens her mouth. And then she stops. She thinks about the first leadership workshop. The workshop facilitator’s words: your role is to create conditions, not control outcomes. She thinks about how she had sat in that workshop and nodded along at all the right moments and then gone back to her office and continued, for months, to hand people the answers they asked for because it was faster and it felt like helping.

She thinks about what Karin asked her, in the circle, when she described Jonas: has it worked?

She thinks about David’s team. Twelve engineers. Very hierarchical, very technical, very convinced this isn’t for them. If she gives David a sequence (step one, step two, step three), he will follow it or he won’t. But either way, it will be her sequence, built for her context, her team, her particular combination of a Jonas and a Sara and a Per. It will not be his.

“Can I ask you something first?” she says. David looks slightly surprised. “Sure.” “What made you come and talk to me just now? Out of everyone in this room?”

He thinks about it. “Something you said in the afternoon session. About the budget process. About deciding to try something before you knew if it would work.”

“What was it about that specifically?” He pauses. “I think… I’ve been waiting until I understood the whole thing before starting. And I’m starting to think that’s not how it works.”

Elena looks at him for a moment. “What’s one thing,” she says, “that you could try next week? Not the whole transformation. One thing. Small enough that if it goes wrong, it’s recoverable. Real enough that it would actually mean something to your team.”

David is quiet. She waits. She has learned to wait and does not fill the silence.

“Well, there is this thing…”

6.1What should you consider before embarking on a transformation journey?#

Through our work with different teams and organisations, we’ve learned that how you approach this journey and what makes you successful at it depends on several factors. It’s important to realise that each transformation journey is, to a large extent, context-specific. This means that, even though we would like to have a clear answer for how to go about it, there is no standard recipe. There are, however, common factors to consider, basic principles that apply across contexts and specific practices that can help you on this journey. Some of these practices have been described in previous chapters, so let us now focus on the factors to consider before starting your journey.

TipMotivate the transformation journey by crafting a powerful story around it that people in your organisation can relate to. You can find inspiration from Fredric Laloux’s book, “Reinventing organisations” (2014), and in this film from his online library: thejourney.reinventingorganizations.com/12.html

The first thing to consider is WHY you, your team, and your organisation want to embark on this journey in the first place. Developing a compelling narrative around why transformation is needed and what its benefits are will help you create the need for transformation within your organisation. In the first chapter of this handbook, we listed some common arguments and possible benefits. What will make your story stronger, however, is anchoring it in a specific challenge that your organisation faces, or a benefit that could arise from introducing collective forms of leadership, culture and the structures for it. To make this narrative even more powerful, complement your organisational motivation with a very personal story that motivates you on this transformation journey.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard doesn’t talk about where to begin, but about what kind of conviction needs to be in place before anything else. The transformations she has seen sustain themselves are those where the impulse to change is deeply tied to the organisation’s actual mission, often because an external pressure or a new strategic direction has made it impossible to continue as before. “It’s not just a communication task. It’s a fundamental conviction that we cannot continue to do what we’re doing now if we are to serve the citizens or the employees.” That conviction must be real, not rhetorical, because without genuine commitment to the underlying purpose, the journey will become cumbersome and people will quietly return to what they know.

Karin Tenelius reflects on the specific question of the public sector, and the importance of connecting the transformation towards leading together to its purpose. Around half of her clients work in the public sector, and she finds it more meaningful than private sector work. The public sector has more tolerance for inefficiency than the private sector, which also means it has more to gain. Its advantage is that it is not driven by quarterly results and tends to carry a genuine sense of purpose, of being there for citizens, for pupils, for improving the health and well-being of communities. That sense of purpose, she argues, is something many public organisations have lost touch with, and reconnecting with it is not just motivationally useful; It’s what makes transformation feel worth the cost. “Municipalities can start to look like ordinary organisations instead of finding their soul again.” And when they do reconnect with that, the scarcity of resources, which is so often experienced as a constraint, can become a driver. When you lack the resources to do everything yourself, you have every reason to activate the community around you, and that activation is itself a form of leading together with citizens.

In more traditional change initiatives within organisations, top management determines the direction and then sets into motion large-scale change processes that are led in a top-down manner. When working with systemic transformation and paradigm shifts in leadership that promote increased participation and co-creation, the top-down approach needs to be complemented with bottom-up initiatives and experiments.

Transformation is not always initiated by top management, even though their support will be needed in the process. When searching for the right allies to support you in driving your transformation journey, a helpful question to ask is, who (individuals or teams) in the organisation would probably be most interested in embarking on this journey? Often, it is known that certain individuals, teams or parts of an organisation are more progressive and open to trying out new things and innovating new ways of working. On the other hand, it is useful to ask, who/which parts of the organisation would benefit most from such transformation? Transformation is more often likely to happen where there is a struggle or crisis, or where current ways of working aren’t providing the right solution to existing challenges.

Another aspect worth considering is that it might be best to start prototyping new ways of working around a task that is close to the core operation of your organisation, but not too complex. Something that is important for the users, and where relevant results could be shown quite quickly. For example, if we were to introduce shared leadership into an elderly care home, we could start with a team that works with a service catered to elderly home residents, for example, the kitchen team or the team in charge of fun activities. It depends on which teams show interest, as well as the need for change towards a shared form of leadership. It also depends on which teams have enabling conditions, such as a supportive manager that wants to initiate a change process and work tasks that benefit from people meeting and working together.

Starting on a small scale and focusing on areas where impact matters allows you to showcase the benefits of this way of working, while creating inspirational stories you can share within your organisation to generate curiosity and interest from other teams.

TipStart your transformation journey on a small scale by engaging only those parts of the organisation where there is interest, energy, or a sense of urgency, and where you can make a significant impact in a short time. It is helpful to work with teams that are close to the core purpose of an organisation, but avoid complex tasks that are difficult to grasp. In this way, you can quickly show results that matter to the organisation and motivate others to follow.

It is very important to follow the process wherever the energy is, rather than spending too much effort trying to change parts of the organisation or individuals who resist the change. It is very human to resist change, unfortunately, as it is far more demanding and energyconsuming to change than to keep doing things the same old way. Most large organisations get stuck protecting and maintaining existing structures and ways of working, rather than being eager to evolve them. This often requires considerable energy from transformation agents. It is not uncommon to see the most driven transformation agents eventually leave the organisation because they are so exhausted from dealing with obstacles and resistance in the transformation process–something we have witnessed firsthand.

Voices from the field

Nati Lombardo’s starting advice is directed at the change agent who does not hold formal power: find an ally who does. Without someone in a position of authority who is genuinely on board, the initiative will remain fragile. Once that foundation exists, her recommendation is to resist the temptation to go wide and instead go deep in one place. Choose a team that already has some spark for this, or a strong culture of trust, or people who are ready to experiment. Try things, see what works in that specific context and culture, and then let it spread from there organically. “You can cross-pollinate to other teams and see what takes hold.” For leaders who want to begin changing their own practice without waiting for an organisation-wide mandate, the same logic applies: bring a different way of being into the spaces you already occupy, propose something to your team, see how they respond, and build from there.

On scaling up, Nati has observed that the most sustainable expansion happens not through top-down rollout but through curiosity. When a team is working in a different way and something is visibly grabbing, useful, and enjoyable, people from other teams start to notice and want to try it themselves. “It expands organically instead of being enforced from the top.” At some point, once several groups are already experimenting, it becomes possible to establish cross-team structures that begin to hold everything together. And at the point where an organisation decides to transition more fully, a larger conversation with everyone becomes necessary: about what the change will actually entail for individuals, what it will look like in practice, and whether people really want it.

Karin Tenelius is candid about the distance between where she started and where she is now in her thinking about how to support these transitions. “I’ve gone from a very naive vision to a more down-to-earth one,” she says. She no longer encourages organisations to pursue complete transformation. She encourages them to be genuinely inspired by self-organising principles and take from them what they can realistically absorb. The conditions matter more than the ambition. Managers need to be not just intellectually persuaded but personally willing to change. If the working climate in a team is unhealthy, that needs to be addressed before new practices are introduced, because no new way of working will take root in soil that is already poisoned. And the starting point should always be the people who are already ready: the pockets of the organisation where there is genuine longing for something different. “Work with people who are engaged and brave. You can find them in any organisation. Build on those people, support them, invest in them, and see the change they can create.”

When those first teams have genuinely shifted, the most powerful thing they can do is become ambassadors, not because someone asks them to, but because they cannot help it. “Two women managers really became ambassadors because they couldn’t help themselves, they found it so valuable.” But that only happens when the results are significant enough to be undeniable. Small improvements do not generate ambassadors. Transformative experiences do. Which means doing everything possible to create the conditions for real results in the first teams before expecting the word to spread. And it’s crucial to find change agents higher up in the hierarchy, because without eventual top management engagement, the work remains–as she puts it–a guerrilla project. Fine as far as it goes, but limited in its reach.

Through our projects, we observed that even the transformation agents with a strong drive and will to initiate the transformation journey, sooner or later, met organisational obstacles that demanded involvement from top management. For example, a manager starts to delegate responsibility to colleagues with the most expertise to represent the team in other parts of the organisation. In this situation, other managers in the organisation start to complain to the formal manager, and top management needs to get involved. Or internal IT systems demand approval for any minor decision from formal managers, which prevents managers from delegating more power and authority to employees. But making changes to the IT system demands the involvement of top management and the IT department.

Top management, especially the CEO, HR, IT and Innovation and development directors might be – in our experience – the most common targets with whom transformation agents will eventually need to collaborate and build alliances. These people are most often involved in driving change initiatives related to organisational culture, leadership, ways of working and organisational processes.

That said, we also observed many cases in our research projects where the transformation journey was not initiated by top management, but by eager individuals. They could be either managers of a specific team or department or simply engaged colleagues who saw the need for such change. These transformation agents then sought the support they needed from top management along the journey.

Therefore, it is important to identify at least one individual from the top management team who can support the journey and serve as the spokesperson for the transformation. Ideally, the whole top management team will be on board and act as a role model in the change process. If top management starts to share leadership in their own team, working strongly together for the benefit of the whole organisation and its ecosystem, it will be easier to spread systemic thinking and shared leadership across the organisation. Unfortunately, that is rarely the case, so transformation agents shouldn’t give up when this is not possible.

When engaging top management in transformation, it is helpful to find connecting points between the transformation initiative and the existing strategy, goals and priorities that top management has established. The aim is to illustrate how moving towards a more shared model of leadership can support the organisational strategy, goals, or specific challenges. For example, one could argue that the transformation could support broader organisational goals, such as increased effectiveness and agility, while also helping to achieve shorter response times to changes happening in an external environment. In addition, it could contribute to lowering sick leave and fluctuation among employees, as well as increasing employee motivation, engagement and well-being. But one could also argue that specific societal challenges that organisations need to address could benefit from this transformation. For example, when a mandate is given to employees in direct contact with citizens–and those employees can have the possibility to quickly respond to citizens and make decisions that better meet their needs– the engagement and well-being of citizens will increase. They will feel heard, understood and empowered to actively participate in decision-making processes that affect their quality of life.

TipHaving an ally in top management is important. Even if you are an employee and only interested in working with transformation within your own team, your team will probably–sooner or later–run into organisational obstacles that demand involvement from higher levels of management and structural support.

In politically steered organisations like municipalities, it’s also important to work together with politicians and craft a narrative about transformation that can support their goals and agendas. Understanding how political cycles operate before, after and between elections, is important for gaining political support at the right time–when politicians are looking for new ideas. You can demonstrate how new ways of working could create support for their programme and goals.

Voices from the field

When Nati Lombardo talks about the role of top management, she is direct: without their involvement, the change will not hold at scale. But the way they engage matters just as much as whether they engage. The worst version is a top-down announcement that the organisation is now going to selfmanage. The better version is top leadership that holds the process rather than driving the outcome– presenting the concept as a question rather than a decision. This approach models the kind of participatory stance that the transformation asks of everyone from the outset. “What would it look like if they held the process: ‘I’ve been considering this, what do you all think?’” That positioning, she argues, changes everything about how the change lands.

When embarking on a journey towards more shared forms of leadership, it is useful to practise self-awareness and acknowledge your personal strengths and weaknesses as a driver of this change process. This should impact both what you focus on during your transformation journey and where you start. It is also informative when it comes to identifying what competencies or aspects of your leadership you need to develop, and who you may want to involve to complement your weaker sides.

Driving a transformation journey towards more participation and collective forms of leadership within your team and organisation will always also be accompanied by a personal transformation journey. Therefore, proactively thinking about your own role–how you need to re-shape it, or what competencies you need to strengthen to be an active agent in this change–is an important aspect of the journey. For example, in our research projects we realised that not many managers are good facilitators of co-creation processes in teams; yet facilitation is a core skill for moving a team towards greater participation. However, there may be other team members who already excel at facilitation, and the manager could ask them to support the transformation process.

TipConsider your role in the transformation and how you may need to develop your own leadership skills to support the transformation journey. Identify people who can complement your weaknesses and bring them along as partners in driving the transformation journey together.

If you are a manager who struggles with working with structures, but you have a natural strength in building caring and trusting work environments, it makes sense for you to start from where your strength lies. Before you start rethinking and redesigning processes to foster more participation within your team, focus on building a strong culture that is conducive to shared leadership. On the contrary side, if you are very good with structures and processes and you work in an organisation that is driven by a lot of rules, structures, and clearly defined processes, it might be smart to start testing participatory meeting structures in your team that will, in turn, increase inclusion, participation and engagement in your team culture.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard insists that leaders who want to initiate this change must fully understand what it demands of them personally before they start expecting it from others. One of the most common pitfalls she sees is leaders who grasp the concept and then assume the work is now for everyone else to do. “If you want to change the organisation, everybody needs to take a critical look at their own behaviour, practice, and habits, and that goes for leaders too.” The middle management layer, she adds, is consistently underestimated and underinvested in these processes. If you want to change a hierarchy, you need the hierarchy to work with you rather than against you.

Karin Tenelius reflects on what the journey has meant for her personally. As a founder and CEO, she has had to learn to trust that her ideas can survive without her defending them, and that being vulnerable and honest about why something matters to her is more effective than using the power she has to push it through. “I’ve learned other ways that are much more collaborative.” And what she has gained is something she describes as immense freedom: the freedom of not needing to control outcomes, write reports, or monitor everything. The freedom of being surrounded by people who are honest with her, and of not needing to be afraid of honesty in return.

Nati Lombardo has spent years supporting other organisations through transitions toward self-management, but she is candid about the fact that the learning has run in both directions. When asked what she has taken from the journey personally, the first thing she names is the sheer amount of inner work involved, something she had not anticipated at the start. “I didn’t really consider that at the beginning. How much I had to look at my own behaviours and patterns.” The personal dimension of this kind of transformation, the need to examine your own habitual ways of operating and the assumptions you carry without knowing it, turns out to be as demanding as anything on the organisational side.

The paradigm shift from hierarchies to more participation and shared leadership involves a complex process. It demands shifting our values, beliefs, worldviews and our understanding of organisations, leadership and ways of working together. It is therefore useful to identify ways to increase awareness of what systemic transformation towards distributed leadership and more self-managed teams means.This includes developing a new vocabulary and shared frames of thinking within the organisation.

Different approaches can be applied to raise knowledge about systemic transformation in your organisation, such as inspirational lectures, which can be integrated into existing forums where managers and employees usually meet. Study circles are another example, where you invite colleagues to read interesting books on systemic transformation and discuss them together. You could also design shorter interactive online seminars that provide small pockets of inspiration and collective learning in accessible ways, or even simply share a podcast episode, video or shorter article with colleagues on a regular (weekly) basis to raise awareness and knowledge on the topic.

Interactive learning formats are more effective, as they offer participants the chance to reflect on what they have heard, seen or read and share their thoughts with other colleagues. Through our research projects, we’ve learned that developing a shared understanding and language around transformation is an important part of the journey.

TipIn parallel with piloting shared leadership in selected teams or parts of your organisation – to demonstrate results and build motivation for transformation–it helps to increase awareness of the transformation towards shared leadership across the wider organisation. Simple ways of inspiring and sharing knowledge with colleagues can spark curiosity and help develop a shared understanding and language around shared leadership.

6.2Things to consider during your transformation journey#

Transformation processes are often messy, complex, iterative and non-linear, which makes them difficult to plan and control. In the various teams we followed during our research projects, we observed that teams would often take a step forward after being energised and inspired by participating in a few workshops, but then something would happen in their external environment. For example, a reorganisation process might be initiated, or a cost-saving and layoff plan launched, which would suddenly create a high level of fear and paralysis in the organisation. Even teams that had started to eagerly engage in a process of transformation might take a few steps back in these situations before they can move forward again in their transformation process.

Transformation agents, therefore, need to be prepared for these setbacks, working with emergence and uncertainty. There will probably be very different opinions circulating about what is being done in the transformation initiative in different parts of the organisation or even within teams that are prototyping practices for shared leadership. This means you must be able to listen to different voices, holding space for various perspectives and complexity, and not see it as a problem. In a constantly changing world we live in–where large-scale changes such as wars or pandemics can occur at any time and impact our society and workplaces–it is more manageable to work with fast prototyping and many shorter iteration cycles. This way, you can continuously adapt and integrate changes in the process as they happen.

Sometimes external changes result in transformation initiatives being completely dropped– and there is nothing we can do about it. We have witnessed a new director enter an organisation and shut down a whole project, even though the project had approved funding, a clear mandate and strong employee engagement. Similarly, if the manager who has initially supported the team to prototype practices for shared leadership leaves the organisation, the work already done can be put on hold. If the new manager is not interested in continuing the process they might stop the initiative, even if other team members want to continue with it. These kinds of changes can be deflating for transformation agents who have devoted a lot of energy and effort to a change initiative, leaving them with the sense that they are starting from scratch. That is why it is wise to prepare oneself for the fact that transformation is a long-term journey that doesn’t have a clear goal and will certainly include many ups and downs along the way. It demands patience, persistence and a long-term perspective.

Voices from the field

There are always setbacks alongside the successes, Karin Tenelius observes. Always forces working to keep things as they are, and always moments that feel like failure. But culture, she says, is like air. It can look completely stuck and then suddenly crack open. “When it looks really horrible and ugly, all of a sudden it cracks and opens. And then that is a genuine breakthrough, a true leap forward.” Which means that the most important quality for anyone doing this work is the capacity to stay connected to the process, to resist the urge to plan everything, and to stay ready for the unexpected. “You don’t have maps, really. You have a few, but very little guidance. You have to stay connected to the process all the time and see what’s happening.”

When employees are presented with new development initiatives, a common response is: “Oh no, another change program! Aren’t we already more than busy with ongoing development initiatives?” This is not a strange reaction. Organisations often have a tendency to continuously initiate new development and change programs, which can be quite stressful for colleagues who already feel swamped with their existing workload.

Therefore, when it comes to creating space and time for engaging colleagues in a transformation journey, a useful practice for transformation agents is to examine the current systems, ways of working, leadership, and culture. That helps to identify what no longer supports the organisation’s purpose, values and priorities–things that could be taken away to create more space for experimenting with new ways of working and leading together.

The process of letting go of old habits and procedures will certainly evoke emotional responses in people who will then need space and time to share frustration, grief and other feelings that may emerge. Hospicing old habits, work practices and procedures that might have been ingrained in an organisation for a long time is not an easy process. Creating connecting rituals that provide space to acknowledge how old habits and practices have served the organisation, and to appreciate them for their contribution, is crucial for preparing people to let them go.

Even as new practices are introduced, it’s important to continually reflect on which ones should be kept and sustained over time and which ones should be removed if they are not working.

Voices from the field

Nati Lombardo suggests that retrospectives are useful for sustaining the practice of leading together over time. The ability to regularly come together and name what is or isn’t working, and continually make adjustments, allows the practice to keep evolving rather than calcifying or quietly dissolving. “At the beginning the ideal can look beautiful, but once you start putting it into practise things emerge, and it’s not so easy. So at least being able to talk about it, to name what’s not there yet, and to keep looking for improvements is key.”

Transformation cannot happen only through people cognitively learning about new ways of working. It must be embodied and accompanied by a deeper transformation process on both individual and collective levels. Creative methods such as storytelling are very supportive in transformation processes. They allow people to open up on a much more personal level, share their own vulnerabilities through stories, and weave deep relationships with each other. Storytelling also allows complex learning processes to be communicated in engaging, relatable and accessible ways. Creative practices inspired by the arts often activate the whole human, including their feelings, senses, imagination, intuition and deeper senses of knowing that are not accessible through language. In our prototyping processes with the public sector, exercises drawn from photography, poetry, improvisational dance and theater, and visual art and collage, have proven to be very supportive. These practices often stimulated creativity among participants and strengthened their courage to explore new things, vocabulary and ways of expressing themselves. They also supported participants in the transformation journey by fostering trust among them and enabling them to connect on a deeper level. Creative methods further strengthened co-creation among participants and supported them in developing some key skills for transformation processes, such as slowing down, deep listening, imagining, improvising, navigating uncertainty and complexity, co-creation and facilitation.

Although participants appreciated the creative methods used in workshops facilitated by researchers and artists, they found it challenging to transfer these methods to their everyday work. Many found ways to do it by adjusting the methods to their own context, using simpler exercises and methods that are more familiar to colleagues. For example, taking photos during a reflection walk with mobile phones and then sharing personal stories about them. Or using music in their own meetings and workshops, and inviting colleagues into simple meditation, dance or energising exercises inspired by improvisational theatre techniques.

Voice from the artistic field

The hardest thing about bringing an artistic practice into an organisational setting, Dejan Srhoj says, is not proposing the exercise. (That part is relatively straightforward.) The hard part is standing behind it, holding space for whatever needs to unfold, and resisting the urge to intervene. “Like a psychotherapist, my role is to hold a space for the creative process to unfold. And I feel the biggest challenge for people who haven’t done it a lot is not to propose, because that’s not so hard, but to stand behind it and to hold the space for whatever has to unfold.” It took him many years to develop that quality of presence: the ability to see very quickly what could have been done differently, what might have helped, and to leave that knowledge unused, because the experience itself is what people need, not the correction.

That is why he sees the natural next step in this work as training people both from artistic and organisational backgrounds not only to participate in co-creation processes around shared leadership, but also to facilitate and hold space for them. This knowledge should be widely distributed, not kept by a small number of practitioners.

But there is another entry point, available to anyone, that doesn’t require specific training at all. Every person already has a relationship with movement. They walk to work, breathe, sleep, go to the gym, play a sport. The question Dejan invites us to consider is, what happens when you shift something small within what you are already doing? What if the 10-minute walk from the underground to the office became a half-hour walk, with stops? What if you ran it as fast as you could? What if you took a different path? What if you walked it with a colleague in silence, or talking loudly? “We are already professionals at walking from the underground to the office, we are already professionals at observing the space. What if I just observed it for a longer or shorter time?” These are not trivial variations. They are scores, in the choreographic sense: simple enabling constraints that change how you inhabit time, space and relationships that generate real information about yourself and others.

The same logic applies inside the workplace. What if the meeting rooms were rearranged? What if the meetings were held in a forest, or conducted while moving? What if there were more time, or less, for a meeting? He describes a score developed by choreographer Lisa Nelson in the 1970s, in which a group closes their eyes and simultaneously creates a movement. What tends to happen is that people begin making the same movement, or nearly the same movement, without being able to see each other. Something in the shared space has been informed in a particular way, and people begin working from it without knowing how. “I don’t know how it’s possible. But there’s something that the space has been informed with in a certain way, and people suddenly start to work from that common space.”

For more extended or demanding processes, facilitation and training are necessary. But for beginning, for shifting something small in everyday life and seeing what it opens, the knowledge required is already in the room.

For those who want to go further and collaborate with artists directly, Dejan’s first piece of advice is to address the prejudices that run in both directions. Artists often carry an assumption that organisations are purely efficiency and profitdriven, indifferent to everything that does not generate output. Organisations often assume that artists are unreliable, working from mysterious inspiration rather than a specific method and knowledge. All these assumptions get in the way of what could be a productive collaboration. “Artists are workers. They have methods, they have a lot of knowledge, they have a lot of references and experiences.” The first step is personal: finding an artist whose work interests you, approaching them, and starting a dialogue. It takes time, and it is not self-evident that it will work. But there must be a basic trust that there is knowledge on both sides worth sharing.

Dejan is also clear that not all art is the same, just as not all organisations are the same. Within choreography, as within any field, there are practitioners who work in deeply traditional, hierarchical ways and have no interest in questioning authority or structure. What matters is finding the right meeting point between a specific artistic sensibility and a specific organisational readiness. “I would not say there is one recipe. There is really a dialogue.”

What he’s observed in the organisations that have engaged with this work is that change tends to begin in small and concrete ways. Managers who participated in the workshops reported learning how important it is to create space at the start of a meeting for people to arrive, slow down, and land in their bodies before the work begins. Some have started reading a poem or conducting a short breathing or grounding exercise with participants. Some have begun rethinking the spaces where they meet, taking their team into a forest instead of a meeting room. Some have shifted their own roles, moving towards the listening structure proposed in the workshops proposed and finding ways to be present and attentive rather than performing and directing. One manager described wanting to give their team time to simply sit in the kitchen for a few hours, observing and listening. “Things are starting to happen,” Dejan says. “But you have to be quite courageous to question these existing patterns in organisations with a lot of established expectations.” The courage required is not dramatic. It is the quieter courage of doing something differently, in a context where the pressure to do things as they have always been done is constant and palpable.

In our research projects we found that a key challenge with change programs or interventions – like those we facilitated during our research process–is sustaining the change effort and integrating new work practices into regular team routines. It is easy to keep high engagement and try new things while external experts come in and support teams with new knowledge and tools. However, once external support is gone, only teams that manage to integrate the new practices into regular team rituals and routines will sustain the change. Transforming culture, leadership and work processes in an organisation is an embodied process that cannot happen if one sends a few individuals to a training course on distributed leadership. Embedding new work practices into daily routines and persisting with them is often difficult unless the formal manager takes a proactive role in setting up new work routines with colleagues and then helps the team sustain them. For example, the manager might remind the team of their shared, co-created purpose, or use participatory meeting structures on a regular basis, rather than only occasionally, for yearly team get-togethers or sessions where yearly development plans are made.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard is firm that this process must be understood as an organisational transformation, not a toolbox. “Organisations that approach this as if it’s a toolbox, you take a course, learn to use the tools, start implementing, and then when that doesn’t solve all the problems, look for another toolbox, that’s a different thing.” If you genuinely think of it as a transformation, approach it that way.

One concrete investment Mette always recommends in the startup phase is developing facilitation skills. Not everyone needs to be trained, but the people who will facilitate dialogues do, and so do the leaders who will be giving them the mandate to do so. Once the process is underway, she recommends having some kind of unit or entity–ideally itself organised through distributed leadership–whose ongoing role is to sense what the organisation needs and respond: developing new training, facilitating new dialogues, creating new tools as the situation demands. And on the question of how far along any organisation is, the most honest answer she has heard comes from organisations that say they do not know, because they are never done. “It’s a constant development journey. You have to love the journey. It’s not a matter of getting there.”

When you initiate the transformation journey with the first teams in selected parts of the organisation, you will start to identify those individuals who are most engaged in the process and want to take on a more active role in driving the change process and spreading knowledge to other parts of the organisation. As you share knowledge about systemic transformation and disseminate learnings from the first pilot teams to the rest of the organisation, you will identify other potential transformation ambassadors. They might show curiosity and choose to initiate transformation within their own teams or parts of the organisation. This is why it is smart to progressively and step by step build a network of support ambassadors or transformation agents, who can drive the movement together and support you in your efforts.

These ambassadors need to build relationships with each other and continue learning how to work with transformation in practice, while also maintaining the energy and motivation required to drive transformation. It is therefore important to create a community among them that offers opportunities to meet and support each other on a continuous basis. These meet-ups should provide both emotional support for transformation agents dealing with pushback and resistance to change within the organisation, as well as opportunities to exchange knowledge experiences, learn new tools, and strengthen relationships and collaboration.

Since existing work spaces often carry the history and culture of an organisation–including specific norms and expectations around what behaviours are promoted–daring to break with current norms and behavioural patterns can sometimes be difficult. Office spaces are often designed in ways that support hierarchy. For example, meeting rooms are commonly populated with a long table and a screen on one side of the room. This results, many times, in classical meeting formats where one person with authority stands in front of the screen and delivers information to the rest of the room that passively receives it for the majority of the meeting time. When we started to experiment with more participatory and creative formats of meetings and workshops, project participants reflected: “How can we do these kinds of meetings and workshops in our own workplace if we don’t have flexible meeting rooms where we can sit in a circle without a table in the middle? We lack bigger, flexible rooms where we can dance, move, paint or build prototyping models and collages together like we’re doing here.”

In the organisations in our projects that had more traditional office buildings, with long corridors and many small rooms, participants experienced a lack of natural spaces that encouraged spontaneous meetings and shared learning between colleagues. The increased amount of online meetings and remote working–and even people from the same team being dispersed across several floors of the office– further exacerbated the situation. It has become hard to create shared rituals and build a strong team culture.

Therefore, to create better conditions for shared leadership, it is important to challenge how existing workspaces are designed and used. For example, simply rearranging the furniture and trying out new set-ups in existing rooms can encourage more participation. Move aside the tables, place chairs in a circle, and add some cosy accessories from other parts of the office (like plants, pillows, and a carpet)–and suddenly, a rather stiff and traditional meeting room is transformed into a more informal and cosy environment where colleagues can relax and open up to each other.

A bigger step in this process is to build a dedicated space or hub for innovation and transformation, which some organisations in our projects have done. These dedicated spaces for experimentation often look more creative than the rest of the office space, and different rules and behavioural norms can apply. Such neutral spaces become a sort of oasis or safe space for experimentation, where new things can be tested without fear of failure. Here, you can host the community of transformation agents through different events and study circles, or invite teams working with transformation and shared forms of leadership to meet, hold workshops, and share their stories and learnings with colleagues from other parts of the organisation. These hubs can be internal and exclusively available to employees, or open to external stakeholders and citizens who are invited to participate in co-creation and transformation processes.

Driving transformation towards greater participation and collective forms of leadership can be challenging–especially when encountering a lot of resistance in one’s own organisation. For this reason, connecting transformation agents from different organisations has proven to be especially nurturing and supportive. Building a shared community through events and workshops fosters knowledge exchange and relationships across organisational borders.

Meeting like-minded people who are on a similar journey can offer strength and inspiration, while helping you feel less alone. It is easy to start doubting yourself if you receive internal critique. Meeting transformation agents from other organisations can provide the emotional support needed to maintain healthy self-confidence and motivation, helping you persist with transformation efforts–even during challenging times.

Voices from the field

Karin Tenelius has advice for the change agents who feel alone in this work–which, in her experience, is most of them. “Form communities across organisations. Create communities where you can find your allies, and gain energy and inspiration from them.” And for those encountering colleagues who are skeptical or resistant, her counsel is to not argue or persuade, but to listen to them, understand what their concerns actually are, let them be where they are, and trust that they will find their way when the time is right. “Respect them, listen to them, and be genuinely interested in what’s bothering them. That’s the way.”

Nati Lombardo adds another piece of advice. This work is complex. It is not a switch you flick. It requires personal involvement and ongoing inner work alongside the organisational changes. But it is also a field with a growing body of collective experience to draw on, and nobody starting out today needs to begin from nothing. “A lot of people are out there trying it. You don’t have to reinvent the wheel. You can build on what all of us have tried in the past. You’re not alone.”

6.3Examples of different journeys#

In this section, we present different case studies from some of the organisations that participated in our research projects conducted in Lidingö, Umeå and Uppsala municipalities. Drawing on collective learning from several organisations, we also created a case that describes how HR as an organisational function can support the transformation from hierarchy towards embodying collective forms of leadership and building culture and structures for more participation. These cases are based on interviews with participants in our research projects who were responsible for implementing learnings from our projects in their own teams and organisations. They illustrate the different paths that teams and organisations might take when exploring the same questions and discovering their own practices for leading together in everyday work. Yet, the basic principles they applied in their transformation processes are shared. We hope their stories inspire you in your own transformation journey.

Case journey

Lidingö’s journey: transforming through culture and ecosystem

+
A clear vision and purpose as the foundationSaying “yes”Building a strong culture for shared leadershipResults of the work doneDistributing leadership beyond the team and into the bigger ecosystem

This case is based on our work with the elderly care department in the Lidingö municipality and an interview with Linda Löfkvist, who leads the department.

Some transformation journeys begin with a mandate or a structural redesign. Linda’s journey began with a question she kept asking out loud throughout her journey: “What kind of organisation do we need to become, and why does it matter beyond our own walls?”

Linda leads the elderly care department within the Lidingö municipality, a small island city outside of Stockholm. What she and her team have built over several years is a living culture, one that started internally and has gradually extended outward into the neighborhood, the city, and eventually to far remote places like South Korea. Yes, South Korea.

A clear vision and purpose as the foundation

Before any ritual, any community initiative, or any structural change, Linda invested heavily in communication as a sustained narrative: painting a picture of what was coming and why their way of working needed to change to meet it.

The picture she painted was honest. Demographic pressure is increasing. The proportion of older people is growing while the working-age population is shrinking. AI is reshaping what humans do and what they need. The traditional service delivery model, where the municipality acts as a provider and citizens as recipients, cannot scale to meet these challenges. Something more distributed, more relational, more co-creative was needed.

She adapted this argument in different contexts depending on the audience. In management meetings, it was strategic. In conversations with her staff, it was personal. At every level, the core message was the same: we are not doing this because we have been told to. We are doing this because the world has changed, and we need to change with it, and when we do it, it creates something better for the people we are here for–citizens.

This investment in shared purpose and a picture of the desired future became the pillars that supported their transformation journey. When people know where the direction is and trust the intentions behind it, they can take initiative without needing to check in at every step. Linda describes this directly: everything that has followed came from people who felt safe because the direction was clear.

Saying “yes”

One of the most consistent observations about Linda from colleagues is that she says yes to almost everything her team proposes. Ideas arrive, often unusual ones, and she does not filter them through an approval logic. She listens, reflects, and finds a way to support.

This is a deliberate practice, grounded in a specific belief: if people bring an idea, they are bringing a piece of themselves with it. Shutting it down sends a message about what kind of organisation this is. Welcoming it, even if it is imperfect or risky, sends the opposite message.

Building a strong culture for shared leadership

The result is that the team has developed a creative set of practices entirely from within. A colleague who is a trained chef organises outdoor barbecues where the team gathers, cooks, and eats together. He thinks of everything: the skewers, the fire, the flatbread. He is not doing this in his administrative role. He is bringing his whole self, and the invitation to do so came from the culture the team has built together with Linda’s support.

On Fridays, they have yoga sessions which are held in a circle and led by staff members. It is not exercise in the conventional sense. Participants check in with themselves, share what they are grateful for, and speak openly about what is difficult. The format creates connections that are hard to develop during work meetings. Attendance is not obligatory, and some people sit on the edge for the first session or two. By the third, something has usually shifted, and people end up joining.

There are “learning books”, small notebooks each team member carries. Meetings often begin with someone sharing a reflection from their learning book, something captured during the week, a high or a low, whatever feels true. They have developed other forms of meeting each other. Walking meetings in the forest. Monthly learning forums where colleagues share what they have discovered, present new ideas, try out new methods, sometimes unfamiliar, like poetry, movement, and new forms of dialogue. These forums are intentionally driven by team members.

What these practices share is that they build the relational tissue that makes shared leadership possible. You cannot ask people to take initiative, give feedback, navigate conflict, and make decisions together if they are essentially strangers who happen to occupy the same department. The rituals create a safe space to build trust, open up to each other and slowly get to know each other on a deeper level–the foundation that helps strengthen collaboration and mutual support in the team.

Results of the work done

The outcomes are visible. Individuals have grown and developed their potential way beyond their formal roles and job titles. Some of the individuals who were quieter and whose voices were rarely heard in group settings before are now the lead facilitators and drivers of change. They have the courage to read their own poems and share stories in front of politicians and other important decision-makers.

Hard-core indicators have also flourished. Sick leave has fallen. Staff retention has improved. Fewer external consultants are needed, and therefore, costs are lower. Colleagues report high levels of motivation, work satisfaction and well-being in yearly workplace surveys. People who work in other parts of the city’s administration have started asking to be placed on Linda’s team, or, if needed, have the opportunity to collaborate with them– even in their free time. Because it gives them energy.

Distributing leadership beyond the team and into the bigger ecosystem

What makes Lidingö’s journey different is how far they have already come. Most teams that embark on this kind of work focus inward, strengthening the culture and capabilities of their own group. Linda’s team did that, and then kept going.

The reasoning was clear to her from the start. The challenges she was working on– loneliness among the elderly, social fragmentation, the disconnection between institutions and communities–cannot be solved by any single department or organisation acting alone. The municipality is one actor in a wider ecosystem. If the goal is resilience and belonging at the community level, then the work has to involve the whole community.

Using GIS data (geodata) to map out where isolated residents lived, the team identified specific neighbourhoods and began building local, self-governing communities in each. These are local communities led by residents who take their own initiatives, and are part of a network connecting them to each other, which the municipality helps to organise.

In Skärsätra, one such community gathers on the first Tuesday of every month for a shared meal. Leo and Kiki, who ran a restaurant until the pandemic forced them to close, cook for the community. Kiki has been blind since childhood and still cooks. The meal this particular evening was polenta, slow-roasted meat and lemon panna cotta, with a proper wine list, for eighty kronor (80 SEK) a head. Neighbours arrived, greeted each other by name, noticed who was missing and went to check on them. A local politician attended at Linda’s invitation. He sat, ate and listened. He is coming back and wants to spread this concept.

This is a community that has found its own form and feeds itself. Linda’s team created the initial conditions and helped it establish its structure. Now it largely runs itself, and the municipality’s role is to stay connected, notice what is needed, and link this community to other parts of the network when it helps and when they need to work together for the greater good.

The network of different actors and local communities that the municipality has built has grown substantially over time. An interactive map covers the entire island: churches, civil society organisations, local businesses, including hotels, and art institutions are all working together. Millesgården, the famous sculpture museum, is now an active participant. When the group identified that some segments of the elderly population were not being reached by anyone, they brought the question to the network and asked who would be willing to take it on. The Red Cross and a local church developed something new together.

The municipality has also encouraged relational bonds and shared leadership across different generations of citizens. On one hand, they placed senior citizens in schools who became friends and mentors to children who were isolated, without friends and struggling with mental health issues. The seniors are warmly welcome. On the other hand, senior cafés at schools run by students were initiated. They are attended weekly by older residents who feel lonely and need company. The students bake, organise activities, and handle the music quiz. The seniors come. One elderly man learned how to use his mobile phone because a student helped him download an app. He was delighted.

Linda describes a phone call she received from a colleague while riding the bus to a hospital. They agreed: this community, all its actors, and all its work, needed to be formally celebrated once a year. They found a date – All Friends’ Day on June 30th. Three years later, the national public health authority adopted it as a national day in Sweden, with other municipalities following their lead and joining the celebration. South Korea and the World Health Organisation (WHO) have shown interest in Lidingö municipality’s work and invited them for exchange and collaboration so they can spread their good practices internationally too.

Working with resistance

Linda does not suggest this journey has been frictionless. The main challenge has been internal: reaching parts of the organisation where managers are accustomed to more traditional patterns and either do not see the need for change or find it threatening.

Her approach has evolved. Early on, she tried to engage everyone. That took a lot of energy without producing much movement. Over time, she shifted her strategy: start where the engagement already exists, work with the willing and the curious, let results build visibility, and trust that the platform creates its own momentum. Working with people who are engaged brings her the joy she needs as motivation in her own work.

When someone who was previously resistant eventually comes around, the response is simple and genuinely welcoming. No bragging about the past results or telling them what they should do. Just: ”What do you think? How can I help?” Begin where you are. This quality of reception–available to anyone regardless of where they are starting, and attuned to their specific needs and situation–is part of what makes her approach work.

The same principle applies at the organisational level. When connecting with a new team or leader, Linda starts with their challenge rather than her method. What are they struggling with? What do they need? The approach follows from that, rather than leading with it.

One last piece of advice

Lidingö’s journey describes, in a powerful way, what can happen when leaders invite colleagues and citizens to be their whole selves, using their passions and potential beyond formal roles or titles. It also tells a story of how focusing on building trust, deep relationships, collaboration and collective leadership–not only within the team and other parts of organisation but also with different stakeholders, including citizens–can have a strong effect on the broader well-being of the whole society. In some ways, it is hard to summarise the Lidingö case because it covers so many interesting examples, stories, and practices, and many of their outcomes cannot be measured only with traditional performance indicators. They have improved the quality of work life for many colleagues, and the quality of life for many local residents by building a strong local community that sustains itself. They have built an ecosystem that connects many actors who collaborate around a shared purpose, which has had an impact on the lives of many individuals.

Linda talks about this in terms of what she believes organisations are ultimately for. The work of building resilience in communities, of weaving connections across generations and organisational boundaries, of creating places where people want to be and feel that they can contribute, is not a side effect of the real work. It is the real work. Everything else–the structures, the rituals, the measurement frameworks–exists to support it.

Her advice to others who want to start is simple: find a community of people who are exploring the same questions, because the sense of shared direction and mutual support is what makes it possible to keep going. Begin with and focus on the curious rather than the skeptical. Create forums for reflection to capture learnings. And trust that what you build, if it is rooted in people’s own energy and initiative, will grow further than you can currently see. Since you will run into resistance and obstacles along your way, connect with other like-minded people from other organisations–even other countries–who can inspire you and keep you motivated. Linda also mentioned that the group of transformation agents in our project, and before that, at Samverket (a hub for co-working and systemic transformation in the Swedish public sector), had that role for her. So find yours.

Case journey

Umeå: a journey of building the new inside the old

+

This case is based on our work with the Strategic Development department in the Umeå municipality and an interview with Mikael Brändström, who leads the department.

When the Strategic Development department was formed under Umeå’s City Management Office, it came with an unusual mandate. Rather than organising the new unit the conventional way, with clear unit managers, defined sub-teams, and a layered hierarchy, the city director at the time gave Mikael and his team permission to try something different. Forty-three people. No fixed hierarchical subdivision below the department level. An open question: Can a public sector unit of this size operate with genuine shared leadership and self-organisation, and if so, what does that actually require?

That question has been the spine of a two-year journey, and the answer (still emerging) has produced some of the most concrete and transferable learnings of our Forte project.

The power of language

One of the first and most deliberate choices Mikael made was to be careful about language. Terms like psychological safety, self-organisation, and self and shared leadership carry weight in some contexts, but in a municipal environment, they can also trigger skepticism, eye-rolls, or the kind of defensive confusion that slows everything down before it starts. Mikael drew on a lesson from an earlier project: the more new concepts you introduce, the more energy is spent on the concepts rather than the change in behaviours and ways of working you are trying to create.

So instead of talking about these concepts, the team talked about what behaviours they wanted in their department. Instead of calling it “culture work”, they called it “working out what kind of work principles they needed in order to function well”. The difference is subtle, but one conversation invites people to discuss an abstraction, and the other invites people to talk about what actually happens when someone takes a phone call in the quiet zone and just keeps talking.

The team landed on working with five key areas, building partly on Laloux’s work on self-managing organisations and finding their own approach to it. These covered decision-making, information transparency, conflict resolution, dynamic roles rather than fixed roles based on job descriptions, and feedback culture as the foundation for continuous development. Each principle was paired with specific behaviours: what does it actually look like when we are living this, and what does it look like when we are not?

This grounding has shaped how they develop the department’s culture and processes over time. Recent staff meetings have focused on feedback, not as a topic to discuss but as something to practice. With the department in the middle of transition to an activity-based workplace, real scenarios come up constantly: how do you give feedback to a colleague who keeps talking on the phone in a quiet zone? What do you say, and how do you say it in a way that strengthens rather than damages the relationship? These are the moments where abstract principles become real.

Mikael was also conscious that they needed to work on the transformation at different levels. This included both strengthening certain skills that were lacking among colleagues and, in parallel, doing things that would change their culture and processes in order to build more structural support for distributed leadership.

Facilitation as the core capability

When Mikael’s team assessed what skills the department would need to strengthen to genuinely change how it operated, facilitation came up immediately. Not facilitation in the narrow sense of running structured workshops, but facilitation as a broad capacity to move groups through complexity, conflict, and change without relying on hierarchical authority to get things done.

They adopted a methodology called Whole Person Process Facilitation, which centres on bringing the full human being into change processes rather than just their professional role. Around 40 to 50 people across Umeå municipality have now been trained in this approach, run in collaboration with a neighboring municipality to spread the cost and build cross-organisational connections at the same time.

The result of this investment has been significant. In most organisations, the manager runs the staff meeting. In Mikael’s department, people trained in facilitation now take responsibility for designing and leading these meetings, without the manager present in a facilitating role. The formal management point still exists on the agenda because it has to: legal responsibilities around workplace safety assessments and similar matters require a designated manager to be accountable. But the format and quality of the meeting itself is the responsibility of facilitators and participants.

This shift matters beyond the quality of the meetings. It is one of the clearest structural expressions of shared leadership available within the constraints of public sector governance. You cannot remove the manager’s legal accountability, but you can redistribute the actual work of leading collective processes to people throughout the team and organisation. And when those people grow into that responsibility, something changes in how they understand their own role within the organisation.

Several of the early facilitators have since become genuinely expert practitioners, and some are now leading training sessions in service design and innovation methodology for others. Capability built in one place spreads to many other places.

Redistributing decision-making through structural changes

Alongside their work on culture and capability, Mikael’s team made a series of structural changes designed to distribute decision-making throughout the department.

The budget process is the clearest example. Traditionally, a department head owns the budget, which includes managing budget allocations, approving invoices and costs, and reporting to leadership. Mikael’s team redesigned the budget process from the ground up. All costs are now transparent and accessible to every member of the department through the financial reporting system, with the exception of individual salary information due to data protection requirements. Together, the team makes an initial budget plan and decides how to distribute resources between different projects and initiatives. Work groups or team members who want to draw on the operational budget submit a request with a priority rating. These are combined into a shared overview, and if the total exceeds available resources, the team works through the prioritization together rather than leaving it to the manager alone.

After each four-month period, everyone who has requested resources does a check: Do I/we still need this? Has the need changed? Can anything be reallocated? The budget becomes a living thing rather than a fixed annual constraint. It also becomes a vehicle for practising transparency and shared decision-making principles the team has committed to.

At the city level, a similar structural reform was introduced recently. Previously, central innovation funding was held in a pool that departments could apply for. This created a dynamic where the departments that did not receive funding felt no incentive to develop. In the new approach, 0.5 percent of each department’s operational budget is directly distributed into innovation, with the requirement that it be used for development and innovation work done together with others. The largest departments receive tens of millions; smaller ones receive less. But the logic is the same: development capacity must be built throughout the organisation, not concentrated at the centre or isolated within departments; it must be exercised in collaboration across organisational boundaries.

Adapting HR processes from within

Not every structural change can be made from scratch. Many of the processes that govern how people are recruited, developed, and evaluated are embedded in municipal-wide HR systems that individual departments cannot simply replace. Mikael’s approach has been to work within these constraints.

The annual development conversation is a good example. In Umeå, as in most public sector organisations, a formal performance and development talk is required by the HR process and must be held by the manager with every employee in their team. That cannot be changed at the department level. But what can be changed is how people prepare for it. Before the formal conversation, every team member now finds two colleagues and has a peer conversation: What do you think I should be focusing on developing, and what have you noticed about where I am strong and where I have room to grow? That input then feeds into the conversation with the manager, making it richer and reducing the dependency on a single person’s necessarily partial perspective. The talk can also be more owned by the employee rather than the manager, who exercises self-leadership in that situation. Questions connected to self-leadership and shared leadership are integrated into the development conversation.

The team has also decoupled development conversations from the yearly salary review talk. When both topics live in the same conversation, the dynamics tend to collapse into something closer to a performance review, where self-advocacy and risk-aversion shape what people say. By separating more continuous development conversations from the annual salary review, the team creates a more honest space where genuine reflection can take place. Development is something that happens continuously within the team, as colleagues support each other through regular feedback sessions.

Recruitment has become a relationship-building tool. When recruiting and onboarding new people, questions related to self-leadership and shared leadership are part of the process. When new team members are onboarded, they are invited to talk about their personal values, passions, purpose and the impact they want to make through their work (using the Personal Canvas, the practice described in Chapter 2) with their colleagues, so they can get to know each other better.

Another practice called Department buddy has been introduced to deepen relationships, not only within the team but also with other departments. Each member of the strategic development unit nominates a partner department they take particular responsibility for getting to know–through periodic informal check-ins, the occasional leadership meeting attendance, and simple presence. The goal is not to be useful in every conversation, but rather to ensure you are not a stranger when collaboration is needed.

The co-leadership structure

As the department grew and Mikael’s calendar filled with steering groups, working groups, and citywide responsibilities, a practical challenge emerged: the formal department head was increasingly unavailable for the kind of informal and relational leadership that makes a self-organising environment function. The solution has been a complementary structure. Mikael holds the formal role of Development Director, carrying external representation and formal accountability. Magnus and Vanja hold unit manager titles, but they function more as the relational anchors of the department. They are present in the kitchen in the mornings and accessible throughout the day, creating the conditions for informal conversation and peer support that the formal structure cannot fully provide. As Vanja has settled into the role over time, the balance has evolved. Mikael describes feeling more genuinely available himself now, as the weight is distributed differently.

The insight is that when one person carries all of the formal leadership, that person’s calendar becomes the bottleneck for everything that requires leadership to happen. Distributing the informal dimensions of leadership to others does not dilute accountability; it creates the conditions for the work to flow.

What has changed and what remains difficult?

The moments that feel most meaningful are often small. A department meeting held entirely by the facilitation team, without the manager needing to drive it, and leaving people feeling it was one of the best meetings they had attended. Overhearing that colleagues are co-creating something, solving a problem on their own, without needing to route it upward. These are the signals that something has actually shifted.

There is also something revealing about the department’s survey results. They are not uniformly better. In some dimensions, scores have gotten harder rather than easier. Mikael reads this as a positive sign: when people develop a stronger sense of agency and a more critical understanding of how the organisation works, they become more willing to name what is not functioning. That is not a failure of the work. It is evidence that people have moved from passive acceptance to active engagement.

The most persistent challenge is the boundary with the surrounding hierarchy. When team members act on the distributed authority they have been given and try to work across the wider organisation in ways that do not follow conventional channels, they sometimes encounter friction from directors and managers elsewhere, who experience this as undisciplined or disrespectful of the established order. Mikael is direct about the fact that this is still the hardest part: protecting a team that has learned to operate differently from a system that was not designed for it.

His approach has been pragmatic. Work most intensively with the parts of the organisation that are open. Do not waste energy trying to force entry into structures that are not ready. Build the relationships and the track record that will gradually shift what others believe is possible.

What are three things worth taking away from this journey?

The first thing Mikael says is that this is not a project. There is no completion date. The work is ongoing as things continue to move, change and develop, and treating it as a finite initiative is the surest way to undo whatever progress gets made.

The second is that the manager has to change personally. Not symbolically, not in terms of how they speak about leadership in meetings, but in actual behaviour: what they choose to decide, what they invite others to decide, where they step back. This is harder than it sounds, and Mikael describes still working on it every day, supported by the honest feedback of close colleagues.

The third is perhaps the most practical. Do not add the transformation on top of everything else. Embed it in what already exists: in how the budget is built, how meetings are run, how development conversations happen, how new people are brought in. If the only time people engage with these ideas is during a dedicated workshop, the ideas will stay in the workshop. The practices need to live in the actual work.

Case journey

Uppsala: a journey of transforming a municipality from within

This case is based on our work with the Development and innovation unit in the Uppsala municipality and an interview with Niklas Huss, who leads the unit.

Some transformations begin with a manifesto. This one began with an elevator and the word “hello”.

When Niklas joined the municipality, he noticed that people walked past each other in the corridors without making eye contact, let alone saying hello. The organisation had a written commitment to being welcoming and inclusive, but the day-to-day reality told a different story. Rather than launching a culture initiative with a name and a logo, he started riding the elevator and asking people how their day was going. The elevator was slow. Nobody could escape. Over weeks, something shifted. A colleague who had worked there for over thirty years and initially pushed back on the idea of building broader connections across teams later stood up in a group session and said: “There is a revolution happening on floor 7.” She had noticed the ripple effects, the changed tone, the small conversations multiplying outward. Her insight: through small steps, you can create enormous change.

Starting with changing the system

A large municipality is, by design, a rigid structure. Legal accountability, equal treatment, and democratic governance all require consistency and process. These are not flaws to be fixed; they are features. But the same structures that ensure fairness can make genuine adaptation nearly impossible. New challenges that cut across departments, problems that live in the spaces between organisational units, systemic issues that require collective ownership rather than individual mandate, all of these tend to get compressed into siloed responses that address symptoms without touching causes.

Niklas’s starting point was to understand complex challenges that the municipality was facing and the political goals related to them. He understood that he needed to connect his ambitions around systemic transformation with those challenges and strategic instruments politicians use to steer public administration. He linked several of the complex social challenges, like “rising insecurity”, “mental health pressures”, “involuntary loneliness”, and “growing disconnection” under the ambition to strengthen social togetherness (social gemenskap) and resilience in the municipality. He succeeded in having this ambition set as a priority in the strategic documents defining the municipality’s budget and goals, with the expectation that different departments would work together to achieve it. The ambition to work with different challenges was already there before him. What was missing was a mechanism to act on them collectively and through a more holistic and systemic lens.

He didn’t start by proposing a transformation methodology. Instead of asking permission to work differently, he created the conditions under which working differently became the expected approach.

Building relationships before building programs

Once the mandate existed on paper, the next move was relational. Niklas began identifying and convening people from across the organisation who were already working on related challenges but were not collaborating. Strategic staff, sustainability coordinators, community development officers, communications leads, facilities managers, and directors of subsidiaries. People who shared an interest in the same systemic problems but whose work was separated by administrative boundaries.

He did not call these gatherings a “leadership development program”. He called them opportunities to explore what they were each doing, finding unexpected synergies, and seeing the system from a wider vantage point. The aim was to shift how these individuals understood their role: not as representatives of their own unit or department, but as co-owners of a larger shared purpose–to make their municipality a thriving place to live in.

The facilitation methods mattered. In one early gathering, he brought a choreographer, commissioned through a national research institute, to lead participants through an experience of a particular neighbourhood that had lost its social infrastructure: a large secondary school with a local library and facilities for community events had closed. The space had gone quiet. The community had fragmented. Participants were invited to move around this large abandoned facility and its neighbourhood in silence, to observe, to feel what was present and what was absent. They shared what they felt and noticed, first in pairs, then in the larger group. The room was filled with strong emotions. Everyone was touched. Roles and titles were temporarily set aside.

This is not the kind of activity that appears in traditional municipal meetings. But it served a specific function: it broke through the professional facades that normally govern interactions between people with different organisational titles and hierarchical positions. Vulnerability, a shared experience, and genuine curiosity became available in a way that a conventional workshop would not have unlocked. Several participants who met in those early sessions later chose to meet and explore new possibilities for working together around shared goals.

From culture to structure

Alongside the relational work of building a network between people from different parts of the municipality, Niklas was building new processes. He identified small pockets of possibilities–places where first experimental projects could be initiated to showcase how the city can work with systemic transformation and more collective forms of leadership. For example, in a residential neighborhood where the closure of the school and library had created visible social deterioration, as mentioned above. He would invite actors across the municipality who could contribute to such pilot projects. This was not a project with a fixed design, but a prototype with an explicit intention to engage participants in emergent processes of co-learning and adaptation. Citizens, local organisations, cultural entrepreneurs, and civil servants worked together to explore what the space could become. A participatory budgeting process was introduced so that residents could help direct how municipal resources were deployed in their own neighbourhood.

In parallel, he developed a new annual process designed to complement the existing budget cycle. The standard budget process tends to produce siloed plans because each unit plans for its own remit. The new process created space for cross-organisational futures work, where staff from different units could co-develop activities together before the plans were finalised. The aim was not to replace the existing system but to add a complementary stream that enabled collective design.

He also invested in developing skills needed in systemic transformation. Staff are trained in capabilities like facilitation, futures thinking, co-design methods, how to hold open processes with citizens, and how to prototype with them rather than plan. These competencies do not advertise their deeper purpose. They simply make people more effective at working together across boundaries.

And Niklas ran an explicit campaign to change the basic social texture of the organisation. The elevator experiment became a formal initiative, a “say hello” campaign, executed not through communications but through the facilities team, which had the physical reach and the logistics to make it real. Coffee was provided at elevator banks. Flyers were distributed. A LinkedIn post he wrote about the initiative was spread broadly and generated conversations across the sector. Small gestures, visible commitment, genuine follow-through.

The leading together structure in practice

Niklas operates with what might be described as a complementary leadership structure. He holds a formal position, but he does not lead this work alone, and he does not position himself as the expert bringing transformation to people. He works alongside others who carry different kinds of authority: political leaders who provide the mandate, administrative directors who provide organisational permission, facilitators and educators who develop specific capabilities, and community actors who bring local legitimacy.

He describes his role with a metaphor he has made official. His title, informally used but genuinely meant, is “human gardener”. He plants, tends, connects, and waits. He does not harvest for himself. The logic underneath this metaphor is that, in a complex system, transformation cannot be installed; it must grow organically. The gardener’s work is to create the conditions for growth, to protect what is fragile, to prune what is crowding out the light, and to trust that the organism knows something about what it needs.

This orientation also shapes how Niklas manages relationships with those who feel threatened by what is emerging. When the existing hierarchy becomes uncertain about what is happening–because things are moving in ways that were not pre-authorised, because people are collaborating in new patterns, because initiatives are gaining momentum outside of normal channels–his response is not to accelerate or to fight. He absorbs the concern. He connects the work back to the official strategy. He invites those who are resistant to participate rather than critique. And sometimes he simply protects the emerging work by not making it fully visible until it is strong enough to withstand scrutiny.

He is direct about the cost of this. Transformation of this kind is not like a smoothly managed project. It is a complex process, emotionally demanding, politically exposed, and often exhausting. He has developed personal self-leadership practices to sustain himself: annual solo retreats where he reflects on whether his inner compass is still calibrated, and engages in meditation, physical practice, and gardening in the literal sense. He does not present these as optional extras. They are part of his personal self-care infrastructure.

What does this journey suggest?

Niklas’s approach offers a specific lesson for those operating inside large, established organisations. Resistance to new concepts is not always resistance to what the concepts represent. Sometimes it is resistance to the label, the jargon, the implicit critique of everything that came before. By not naming the transformation, by letting it grow through structural changes, new skills, relational investments, and small visible experiments, Niklas made it harder to argue against and easier to join.

The framework was never the point. The relationships were the point. The new processes were the point. The capabilities people built were the point. The shift in how people understood their own agency, their own mandate, their own role in the larger system, that was the point. A stronger local community and the well-being of citizens was the point.

By the time anyone might have thought to ask what this approach was called, it was already producing results that were difficult to dispute.

Case journey

A journey about how HR can support leading together

Most transformation efforts fail not because the ideas are wrong, but because they never make it into the everyday systems that govern how an organisation actually operates. Job descriptions, recruitment, onboarding, performance and salary reviews, training and competence development, leadership profiles, forums and programs: these are where culture is either reinforced or quietly contradicted. This journey is about how a few HR strategists participating in our projects set out to close that gap.

Developing new language and shared frameworks

In several participating organisations, terms like self-leadership or trust-based leadership already existed in their strategic documents, business plans or policies. But rarely was it clear what these terms really meant and how they could be embodied in culture and ways of working. Very little had been done in practice. Some of the HR strategists began exploring how they could support transformation through small steps, attempting to build an infrastructure without waiting for a perfect mandate or a top-down directive.

One of the first challenges was definitional. For example, self-leadership means different things to different people, and when a concept means everything, it ends up meaning nothing in practice. Providing simple frameworks around key concepts, such as self-leadership, and anchoring them in existing research was one of the things the HR practitioners in our project did. For example, they defined self-leadership in three dimensions: self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-direction. This gave managers and employees a shared vocabulary–a way to talk about something that had previously been vague and easy to dismiss.

Making those definitions accessible and visible to employees and managers on the intranet was another step in this process. But posting content on the intranet changes almost nothing on its own. The real work, as some of the HR practitioners said, was getting in front of people.

They attended leadership meetings and forums across different departments and support functions. They led introductory sessions about basic terms that combined theory and practical exercises, with some of these sessions being in person, others remote, depending on how each part of the organisation operated. After addressing the management layer, similar conversations were brought to all-staff meetings, which required persistence. Not everyone was immediately enthusiastic. There was resistance, competing priorities, and the usual organisational inertia that greets anything that does not fit neatly into existing routines.

From self-leadership to leading together

One insight from this process that HR practitioners shared was that self-leadership cannot be treated purely as an individual matter, even though organisations often push for self-leadership, placing more pressure on individual responsibility. When people encounter the concept only in a lecture, course or article, it tends to stay abstract. When a team explores it together and figures out how team members can support each other in practising self-leadership, something different happens.

When people started reflecting on their own needs and working styles alongside their colleagues, they learned things about each other that had not come up before: someone’s need for recovery time, someone else’s preference for how to receive feedback. These disclosures built trust, and trust created the psychological safety that made further change possible.

An exercise that became particularly popular in one organisation is called My Ideal Work Week. Teams worked through questions about how they actually wanted to structure their work time, where they were losing energy, and how they were prioritising. What emerged was often surprising: people discovered that their colleagues had very different needs and rhythms, and that surfacing these differences was not destabilising but relieving. It gave teams permission to talk about how they worked, not just what they were working on. It supported them in co-creating shared agreements on how they could work better together.

Supporting teams in developing new ways of leading together

Another thing the HR practitioners in our projects tested was away from work limited by policies and standard HR processes, and towards more customised support for managers and teams. When teams struggled, the HR practitioners offered to facilitate workshops on topics that could help improve team dynamics and nudged members to practise more shared leadership–without necessarily labelling it as such. For example, they might focus on discussing shared values and purpose, exploring the team’s experience of psychological safety and ways to improve it, or agreeing on how to work better together and share responsibilities for team rituals and meetings.

Similar support was provided by some of the internal development units in participating organisations. In one organisation, where the development unit participated in our project, some of the developers who work with different teams in their organisation began integrating methods they’d learned from our project into their own work with teams. In this way, practices that support shared leadership were spread to different parts of the organisation.

Development programs for managers and employees

One HR practitioner developed a structured programme for the managers on creating conditions for self-leadership, which was designed and delivered in partnership with an external provider. The programme runs over seven to eight months and combines formal learning with peer learning groups and ongoing network sessions. Around 60 managers have already completed it. Multiple participants have described it as having changed not only how they lead their teams, but how they approach their own lives. The goal is that every manager will go through this programme eventually. To sustain the learning, an alumni structure with two gatherings per year was developed, keeping the cohort connected and continuing the conversation over time. The logic is simple: a single training experience fades; a community of practice does not.

In another organisation, a few facilitators from the development unit developed a Leadership program for non-managers, spreading the idea that leadership is a shared practice that needs to be distributed across the organisation rather than limited to formal management. In this way, they tried to integrate knowledge and methods from our project with their existing methods for leadership development and disseminate them across the organisation. The programme was so popular among employees that they ended up making it a more regular training programme.

The challenge with such training programmes is that they cost money and time. The economic and time pressure on short-term deliveries can often prevent managers and employees from accessing relevant training. This is a structural constraint that needs to be worked around. One way to solve this is to use internal staff to deliver such training programmes rather than hiring more expensive external consultants. In our projects, we saw that especially development and innovation units had colleagues with strong facilitation skills who were the first to go out to organisation and support individuals and teams with training and competence development. Sometimes it is possible to find alternative forums for learning, such as self-organised study circles. To run those, HR or development functions can provide initial support and train ambassadors, which in turn then help spread knowledge by initiating such study circles. They can be run as extended lunch conversations to make them more easily accessible to colleagues who feel pressured by time.

Bringing collective leadership and a wider systemic transformation to senior roles

From supporting individuals and teams with strengthening their self and shared leadership skills, some HR practitioners found ways to advance the larger topic of systemic transformation and the distributed leadership needed to support it among top management. In one organisation, the senior management team began discussing transformational leadership after being exposed to guest speakers at their annual leadership forums facilitated by HR. Evaluations showed a measurable shift in awareness: managers who had been exposed to the concepts once returned a year later, noticeably more prepared to engage with them.

Afterwards, transformational leadership was integrated into shorter and more regular monthly leadership sessions as a theme, inviting internal and external speakers to highlight different aspects of leading during transformation. Not every manager arrives convinced. In early sessions, some participants were visibly dismissive of what they were hearing, regarding it as another management trend. Changing how people understand leadership requires repeated exposure over time, as well as concrete examples demonstrating the difference it makes, and enough time for skepticism to give way to genuine curiosity.

Rebuilding HR processes from the inside

The point HR practitioners have been raising in our project is that lasting change requires making changes to the systems themselves, not just the programming around them. HR processes–the ones that govern how people are recruited, welcomed, developed, and evaluated–encode assumptions about how organisations work. If those processes are built on mechanistic top-down principles that remain unchanged, they will undo whatever the training and workshops are trying to build.

In recruitment, some organisations from our projects are rethinking what they need to look for in new employees and how interviews should be structured. This is so that the candidates who understand and can operate in more participatory environments and self-managed teams are more likely to be identified and selected. In onboarding, some organisations have integrated practices related to self-leadership and shared leadership, or built transformation thinking into how both new employees and new managers are introduced to the organisation.

The performance conversation process is also under examination in some places. The traditional structure relies heavily on manager-to-employee feedback, which reflects a hierarchical assumption: the person above you knows best what you have achieved and what you should develop. This is worth questioning, as the colleagues who work alongside each other daily often have richer insight into each other’s contributions than a manager who is responsible for many people and further removed from the day-to-day work. Moving towards more collective feedback practices–where peers give each other input regularly rather than waiting for a formal annual conversation– would change not just the format but the underlying relationship between accountability and leadership.

Transforming the HR function

Some HR practitioners in our projects acknowledge that supporting systemic transformation from hierarchies towards more participation and collective forms of leadership demands changes in how we understand HR as a support function. The traditional HR role is oriented towards top-down policy and standardisation of HR processes, often delivering finished answers on how things should be done. In a global world where the public sector must operate in a fast-paced, complex, and ever-changing environment, the role of HR is evolving. HR needs to learn how to hold space for tough questions and conversations, build systems that support reflection and experimentation, facilitate collective processes adjusted to specific needs and contexts, and build networks of people across organisational boundaries rather than only supporting vertical relationships within them.

The capabilities HR practitioners in our projects consider most important for them to develop in the future are facilitation, organisational design thinking oriented towards systemic logic rather than hierarchy, and a learning mindset that treats failure as information rather than as something to be avoided. People analytics and the use of AI tools are also crucial. These technologies will increasingly help HR understand both the bigger picture of the whole organisation within its larger ecosystem and the specific needs of individuals and teams.

To those considering taking a similar path, the HR professionals in our projects offer this advice: Start small. Pilot things. Do not wait for perfect conditions or full organisational buy-in before beginning. Lobby upward without giving up when you meet resistance, because resistance is a normal part of introducing anything genuinely new. Find colleagues and mentors who can help you carry the weight of the work and make sense of what you are learning. Accept that the change you are working towards will not happen overnight. Sustaining the effort requires recognising and celebrating even small progress made along the way. The goal is working towards an organisation that has developed the capacity to function well while change is the normal condition, not something that occasionally disrupts a stable baseline. Building that capacity is ultimately what HR is for, they advise.

6.4From working with changes within existing organisations to developing distributed leadership across ecosystems#

Depending on your role, decision-making power and ambition, as well as the organisational conditions, maturity and interest of your colleagues, you will determine the best way to begin your transformation journey. It might begin with a smaller initiative in your own team where you can most easily prototype practices for self- and shared leadership. Perhaps you are part of top management or working with innovation, development or HR issues, and you can have a more ambitious plan. No matter how small or big you will start, we hope this book has provided you with some knowledge, concrete practices and tips based on experiences of others who have already been on this journey.

As mentioned several times in this book, we have seen that rather than starting big from scratch, it might be smarter to find smaller pockets of possibility where there is a need, curiosity and engagement. While supporting organisations on their transformation journeys from hierarchical structures towards greater participation and leading together we have observed that there were two main directions these transformations would take.

On the one hand, when working with transformation within existing teams and departments, the pilot teams we worked with began to increase trust and psychological safety, and build culture, skills and processes that supported transformation towards more self-managed teams. When first success stories surfaced within existing teams, interest from other parts of the organisations followed naturally. Support functions such as HR or development and innovation were used by several participating organisations to disseminate knowledge and methods to other interested areas.

On the other hand, in organisations that either reached higher maturity levels in the first pilot teams (like in the case of Lidingö municipality) or where the development and innovation function got the mandate to drive transformation (like in Umeå and Uppsala municipalities), a more systemic approach was developed where self and distributed leadership were not only developed within existing teams but rather spread in their wider ecosystems, connecting different parts of their organisations with external stakeholders, including citizens. The latter required that transformation agents invest their energy in building new networks and relationships among different actors, bringing them together around complex societal challenges that demanded greater collaboration across the ecosystem. For example, in Lidingö, efforts focused on the challenge of sustainable and preventive elderly care; in Umeå, around the challenge of increased youth criminality and decreased security among youth; and in Uppsala, around the need for stronger social togetherness and resilience in local communities. All participating organisations that managed to work with systemic transformation–from hierarchies to participation and distributed leadership–in their ecosystems also invested significant energy in anchoring the need for systemic transformation with politicians and top management. This helped create not only bottom-up engagement among colleagues, citizens and external actors, but also helped link transformation to overall policy, strategy and prioritised goals on organisational level.

They worked to create safe spaces for experimentation, such as internal innovation hubs where transformation agents from different parts of their organisation could gather, learn, and try out new things together. In this explorative, co-creation process, these organisations dared to use creative methods inspired by design and artistic practices to engage participants in a more holistic way.

6.5Questions for reflection#

  1. What is your personal motivation for working with transformation towards greater participation and more collective forms of leadership in your team, organisation, or in the wider ecosystem?

  2. Why does your team, organisation, or the wider ecosystem need this transformation?

  3. What could the benefits of this transformation be for your team, organisation and/or the wider ecosystem, including end users?

  4. Where and how could you and your colleagues initiate this transformation journey?

  5. Which teams or parts of the organisation would be interested and benefit most from stronger collaboration and collective forms of leadership? Where could significant results be produced in a rather short time?

  6. Is there a complex challenge your organisation is facing that would benefit from stronger participation, collaboration and shared leadership? What is it?

  7. Who from top management could support the transformation and help you anchor it within top management (and with politicians if needed)?

  8. How could strengthened participation, collaboration and distributed leadership support the organisation’s overall strategy and goals, or help solve challenges your organisation is facing today?

  9. In what ways could it be possible to create interest and raise general awareness and knowledge about systemic transformation towards more participation, collaboration and distributed leadership in your organisation?

  10. Who could be your allies in helping you raise general awareness and knowledge?

  11. What are your key strengths and challenges as a transformation agent? How can you use your strengths, and who could you engage to help you navigate your weaknesses or challenges in this journey?

  12. How does your current office space support and/or hinder collaboration, co-creation and shared leadership?

  13. If your workplace lacks spaces for co-creation, are there minor interventions you could implement in the office, or other pockets of possibilities you can identify, to create enabling spaces for co-creation?

  14. What might the key obstacles in your transformation journey be, and how could you work around them?

  15. Which other organisation(s) or communities could you learn more from about systemic transformation towards participation, co-creation and collective forms of leadership?

6.6Conclusions#

In this chapter, we introduced basic principles and tips to consider when designing your own journey as a facilitator of systemic transformation within your team or organisation– towards increased participation, co-creation and collective forms of leadership–in order to better navigate complexity. We shared several case studies from participating organisations to provide concrete examples that can hopefully inspire your own journey. We also investigated challenges that other organisations and experts have experienced and offered suggestions on how to work around them.

This entire book has been a journey in which you were invited to explore what systemic transformation–from hierarchies towards increased participation, co-creation and leading together–entails, along with its benefits and challenges. We examined how the individual, cultural, and structural levels are all key building blocks to keep in mind simultaneously throughout the transformation process. At the beginning of the book, we explored the importance of self-leadership on an individual level and how to create collective conditions that support self-leadership development in organisations. We explained why self-leadership must be complemented by a strong culture of trust, safety, inclusion, learning and mutual support to enable collective forms of leadership. After addressing the need for a supportive culture to lead together, we focused on the changes in roles and competency development required for both managers and employees in this transformation journey. Lastly, we introduced participatory processes and structures that can support shared leadership.

We hope you now feel inspired and empowered to jump in and initiate your own experiment in practising more self and shared leadership in your workplace. We wish you the best of luck on this journey! Please don’t hesitate to get in touch if you need help. We will continue to hold space for individuals, teams and organisations who are on the shared journey of systemic transformation towards leading together.
