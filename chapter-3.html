{elena’s story}

Elena is attending a new workshop run by karin about culture and relationships. They have been doing some movement exercises that feel nice. Now, Karin invites participants to sit in a circle and asks Elena to describe her team.

Elena describes Sara’s precision, Jamal’s instinct for relationships, and Per’s reluctance that sometimes conceals his best thinking. Then she mentions Jonas.

She describes him the way she always does: “talented, full of ideas, still finding his feet.” She mentions the coffee morning proposal, the a3 diagram, and the book club. She can hear herself use the phrase, “I’ve been trying to protect him a little from how slow the system moves.”

Karin doesn’t say anything immediately. This is, Elena has noticed, a habit of Karin’s. The pauses are not empty: they have a texture, weight.

“Protect him from what, exactly?” Karin says. “From…discouragement, I suppose. From hitting bureaucratic walls and losing motivation.”

“Has it worked?” Elena opens her mouth. Closes it. She thinks about Jonas at the coffee machine. The polite smile. The notebook he barely writes in anymore. The way he used to stay late, but now leaves at exactly five o’clock.

“No,” she replies. “Not really.” Karin nods slowly. “What do you think he needed instead of protection?”

Elena looks out the window. It’s a grey Tuesday in November. A tram goes past.

What did he need? He needed the system to be less rigid. He needed Elena’s director to create space for new initiatives. He needed the bud-get cycle to work differently, the restructuring not to happen, the sub-mission guidelines not to require a specific Microsoft Teams folder. He needed things that were not in Elena’s power to give.

But then she hears herself, and the excuse in it. She thinks about what she actually said to Jonas. “Great idea. These things take time. Keep the ideas coming.” She had never once sat down with him and said: “Here is how this organisation actually works. Here is what makes a proposal land. Here is what I would do differently if I were you.” She had never offered her real knowledge.

“I think he needed me to actually tell him the truth,” she says to Karin. “About what I see in him. And about what I think he’s missing.”

“What do you see in him?” “Someone with genuinely good instincts who doesn’t yet know how to make them land. Who’s proposing the right things in the wrong register. Who’s waiting for the system to recognise him instead of learning how to move inside it.”

After the workshop, Elena schedules a conversation with Jonas for the following Thursday. She thinks about it more than she expects to.

She is not worried about Jonas’s reaction. She is worried about something harder to name: that the conversation will require her to admit, implicitly, that she has not been fully present as his manager. That the kindness she’s been performing has been partly for herself (a way of being the good one, the supportive one, the one who didn’t add to his frustration) while quietly avoiding the discomfort of the real conversation. She has been managing her own feelings. And calling it mentorship.

One week later, Jonas and Elena sit in a small meeting room on the second floor.

“I want to be honest with you about something,” she says. “I think I’ve been letting you down.”

Jonas looks up. Carefully. “Your ideas are genuinely good. The a3 diagram, the coffee mornings. They’re not just nice ideas, Jonas, they’re right. They’re the kind of thinking this department actually needs.” She pauses. “But I’ve never told you what I actually observe. How you’re presenting them. What I think you’re missing. I’ve just absorbed your frustration on your behalf and told you to keep going. That wasn’t fair to you.”

The room is quiet. “What do you think I’m missing?” Jonas says. His voice is careful. She tells him. Not everything at once. But the true things: that he’s proposing before he’s built any allies, that his emails read as solutions when they should read as questions. She tells him what she would have done differently. She tells him what she sees that he doesn’t see about himself yet.

It’s awkward in the way that honest things are awkward. There are moments of silence that require sitting with. But Jonas doesn’t deflect. He writes things down. At one point, he says, “I thought you just didn’t think my ideas were viable.”

“I should have said this months ago,” Elena says. “I’m glad you’re saying it now,” Jonas says. Elena nods. Then, after a moment: “There’s something else I want to tell you. Not just about the feedback.”

She had not planned this part. But the conversation has created a kind of honesty that feels worth staying inside.

“I’ve been thinking a lot lately about how we work as a team. About the fact that your ideas keep hitting walls not only because of how they’re framed, but because there’s no real structure here for them to land in. No regular space where we actually think together about what we’re trying to do and how. Where someone like you can bring an idea and have it genuinely explored instead of forwarded upward and buried.”

Jonas is listening more openly. “I’m working on changing that. Not dramatically. I don’t control the budget cycle or the restructuring. But the things I do control: how we run our meetings, whether we have real conversations about what’s working and what isn’t, whether there’s a ritual in this team for actually hearing each other.” She pauses.

Jonas looks at her for a moment. Something in his face is doing the same thing it did in that first week, when he spread the a3 diagram across the table. Not quite hope, but the precondition of it.

3.1Why culture shapes how we lead#

Moving towards more collective forms of leadership requires more than a structural reorganisation. It demands a fundamental transformation of the organisational culture. While frameworks and processes provide the structure for sharing leadership, it is the cultural foundation that determines whether such models thrive and exist in practice. Culture, in this context, encompasses the often-invisible patterns of interaction, the unspoken norms about how people relate to one another, and the collective practices that either enable or constrain genuine collaboration.

Hierarchical leadership structures have long relied on formal authority, clear reporting lines, and the assumption that decision-making flows from the top down. More collective forms of leadership, by contrast, require a foundation built on relationships where authority emerges from expertise, adapts and changes to the situation at hand, and is based on a shared understanding rather than hierarchy alone. This transformation cannot succeed through structural changes alone; it requires cultivating new ways of being together.

The relationship between culture and collaborative leadership works both ways. Collaborative practices can help reshape culture, but without intentional cultural work, hierarchical patterns resurface even within supposedly equal structures. Organisations may implement shared decision-making processes only to find that the same voices dominate, conflicts remain unaddressed, and power dynamics continue unchanged. Culture provides the ground upon which new leadership practices either succeed or fail. Building a culture for leading together requires sustained effort over a longer period of time, as changing culture demands changing people’s norms, hidden assumptions, behaviours and daily rituals of being together. It takes time.

If you want your team to lead more collaboratively, focus on strengthening the cultural areas described in the following section. For each topic, you’ll find exercises and readings to guide your practice.

3.2Creating psychological safety: The foundation for leading together#

Identifying areas for development to strengthen a culture of shared leadership.

At the heart of any collective form of leadership culture lies psychological safety – the shared belief that the team is safe taking risks with each other. Amy Edmondson’s research demonstrates that psychological safety is not about being nice or comfortable, but about creating conditions where people can speak up, challenge ideas, admit mistakes, and bring more of themselves to collective work. For example, by being able to work with questions and areas they want to develop. Edmondson’s research emphasises the role of the leader in psychological safety to reinforce important dimensions and behaviours. In collective forms of leadership, this leadership role may be shared among multiple people.

Psychological safety rests upon several connected elements. Trust forms the foundation – not blind trust, but earned trust built through consistent behaviour and reliability. When team members trust one another, they can engage authentically rather than pretending or being strategic. Inclusion ensures that diverse perspectives are not merely tolerated but actively sought and valued, recognising that collective forms of leadership require drawing on collective intelligence. Supporting each other through challenges and setbacks creates resilience, allowing teams to navigate unavoidable difficulties without falling apart. Finally, a willingness to take risks – to propose unconventional ideas, experiment, fail and learn – enables innovation and adaptation in collective forms of leadership.

Critically, psychological safety also requires having the courage to engage in open conversations to address conflicts and challenges directly rather than avoiding them. In hierarchical cultures, disagreements often get suppressed or redirected upward to authority figures for resolution. In collaborative leadership cultures, the ability to engage productively with conflict becomes essential. This means developing the collective capacity to name tensions, explore differing viewpoints, and work through disagreements without damaging relationships. The alternative – conflict avoidance – leads to surface-level agreement while real issues remain hidden underneath, ultimately undermining both relationships and results.

Voice from the artistic field

In our research projects, we employed artistic practices to help build psychological safety among participants. Choreographer Dejan Srhoj reflects on how a choreographic practice that emphasises investing time to tune in before people start working together – by slowing down and bringing awareness to space, time, relationships, and feelings – strengthens different aspects of psychological safety, including trust, taking risks, inclusion and open conversations:

“Before work happens in a room, something else has to happen first. Where am I physically? What is this space? What is its temperature?How large is it? Do I feel safe here? Do I feel welcome? Who are the people around me, and how do I relate to them? Where did I come from before this meeting, and where am I going afterward? These are not trivial questions to be ignored to start the ’real’ work. They are the real work, or at least the necessary ground for it. And they take time.”

In dance and choreography, Dejan says this tuning is so fundamental that practitioners do it without thinking. It is the default, the starting point of every rehearsal, something nobody questions. In his work with organisations, however, he discovered that this is far from the default elsewhere. “People simply sit and start talking, as if you can jump over all of this just because you told yourself,: let’s just start working. But it doesn’t work like that.” The body is still in transit, still arriving, still negotiating the room and the people in it, while the meeting has already begun.

What choreography brings, then, is an understanding of dramaturgy: the thinking about how an event is structured in time and space. When do people move, when do they reflect, and when do they rest? How is the space organised? How much time is devoted to each phase, and in what sequence? “Everything is important: when you have the break, what kind of break it is, when you reflect, how you reflect, when you move and for how long.” This is nothing to be stressed about, but rather, something to be attentive to. And the answers might differ from time to time, from place to place. Recognising how people inhabit a shared space and time shapes everything that becomes possible within it.

The effect of understanding and attending to this, Dejan says, is trust. Not trust as an abstract value to be stated or agreed upon, but trust as in something that actually arises in a body, in a room, and between people. And from trust arises something else that organisations rarely make room for: playfulness. “We’ve established trust, so we can be playful. And within that playfulness, different qualities of people can emerge, some of them that we know and some of them that are not known.” Playfulness allows for silliness, for not knowing, for failure. It also creates something that is still rare in organisational life: a more equal starting point. When nobody already knows how to do the task at hand, when everyone is practising something new together, the hierarchy slowly dissipates.

What this setup ultimately makes possible, and what Dejan holds as a deep intention in this work, is the possibility of a whole person being present. Not the rational or the professional role alone, but the whole individual, the body, the emotions, the difficulties and desires. All the parts of a person that workplaces usually ask to be left outside the door. The embodied experience is meant to be brought into dialogue with the cognitive: how does what has just emerged in me translate into my everyday work and life? That translation, Dejan acknowledges, is where the biggest challenge lies. Some of it happens consciously, through reflection. And some of it, perhaps the biggest part, happens through continuous practice and without quite knowing how.

Practice

A practice for psychological safety#

Evaluate: Review the four dimensions of psychological safety described above – inclusion, supporting each other, taking risks, and open conversations. Together with colleagues, discuss each dimension: how developed is each one in your team?

*Validated self-evaluation tools are also available in Edmondson’s book The Fearless Organization (2019) that can be used to supplement this discussion.

One approach is to rate your group on each dimension, either as a whole or individually, using a traffic light system (green,yellow,red) to identify areas for development. Discuss why you’ve given each dimension this rating and what specific behaviours or patterns make it that colour.*

3.3Listening and dialogue: communication that enables us to lead together#

At its core, leading together depends on the quality of conversation and collective thinking that occurs among team members. This requires moving beyond conventional debate, where participants argue for pre-decided positions, towards genuine dialogue where understanding and collective insight can develop. This collective insight creates a richer, more complex understanding and can build shared ownership before decisions are made.

Downloading – listening for what confirms what we already know – keeps us stuck in existing patterns. We appear to be listening to the other person while talking to them to seem polite while, in reality, we are more focused on our own agendas and getting our point across. Factual listening helps us open our mind and stay open to information that challenges our assumptions. We listen for information and facts in what the other person is saying, but also already look for cracks in those facts that we can argue against when it is our time to speak. This way of listening, which often results in debate, is typical in an academic setting. Empathic listening allows us to see from another’s perspective. When utilising this kind of listening, we step out of our own patterns of thinking to listen to the other person with an open heart, putting ourselves in their shoes and understanding their perspective. Generative listening, the deepest level, opens us to emerging possibilities that weren’t present before the conversation began. We can follow the flow of conversation and sense what needs to be said, co-creating with others in a way that helps us expand our own ways of thinking in a generative way. Collaborative leadership requires building the ability for these deeper forms of listening to take place across the organisation.

Distinct from discussion or debate, dialogue creates space for collective thinking. Rather than individuals presenting finished thoughts for approval or rejection, dialogue allows individuals’ ideas to be explored, built upon, and transformed through interaction. Key principles of dialogue include noticing and suspending judgment, making one’s thinking and reasoning process transparent, and allowing enough silence and reflection for new insights to surface.

Practice

Dialogue practice#

In dialogue practice, as described by David Bohm (1996), a group intentionally practises dialogue to learn this new way of being together. Continuous practice is essential – dialogue is a skill that develops over time through repeated experience. For instance, a group might set aside two hours weekly to practise dialogue.

Setting up a dialogue practice:#

  • Sit in a circle so that participants can see each other (groups of 4 – 20 people work well)
  • Begin with a brief meditation or silence to help everyone settle into a listening space – ask participants to tune into their bodies, breath, and the space itself
  • Start the dialogue. In the beginning, we recommend having a facilitator who can help the group slow down and notice the quality of conversation

Guiding principles:#

Ideally, dialogue has no predefined question or agenda. This allows the conversation to follow what wants to be explored by the group in the moment – different topics can be shared. However, for a first dialogue session, it can be helpful to focus on the topic of dialogue itself: What is dialogue? How is it different from a discussion or debate?

Meta-dialogue:#

To learn more, explore these additional resources: Scharmer, C. O. (2018). The Essentials of Theory U: Core Principles and Applications. Berrett-Koehler Publishers.
Bohm, D. (1996). On Dialogue. Routledge.

An important aspect of dialogue is meta-dialogue, that is, the group commenting on the conversation itself. Notice: How spacious does the conversation feel? If people are interrupting, waiting eagerly for their turn to speak, or raising hands, these can be indicators that participants are focused more on speaking than listening. When you notice these patterns, name them, slow down, and invite people to tune back into listening.

Movement score

Movement score: group morning exercise on dialoguing (30 min)#

  1. As you walk through a space, notice the walls, objects, and other people. Notice your breathing and relax your shoulders and neck and jaw. Release your feet to the ground. Stretch your body parts as you walk. (5 min)
  2. Find a partner. Your partner will move through the space and also up and down. Your task is to follow the height of your partner’s pelvis with your own. That means that when your partner jumps, you jump, and when they move towards the ground, you follow. You might be close to each other or far away in space. The idea is that you become so connected in movement that an outsider would not be able to discern who is leading and who is following. (5 min)
  3. Exchange roles and repeat the exercise. (5 min)
  4. Now, both you and your partner are leaders and followers at the same time. So the leadership role will shift instantaneously. Do this for at least 10 minutes. You might want to put on some background music.
  5. Give each other feedback on how it felt, what the challenges and joys of dancing together were.

3.4Building a culture of feedback, coaching and conflict resolution#

In traditional hierarchies, feedback and coaching typically flow downwards from managers to employees. Often, feedback and coaching are limited to yearly performance and development reviews, and, at best, such talks occur between managers and employees a few times a year. Collaborative leadership requires a fundamental shift – spreading out these practices so that feedback and coaching flow in all directions, becoming informal, continuous, and built into everyday work. This shift is essential because the responsibility for developing people and holding them accountable can no longer sit with just one manager – it must be shared across the team. As people navigate changing roles and participate in shared decision-making, they need ongoing feedback from their peers to understand how their actions affect others and to develop the group’s collective competence and abilities to work together. This peer feedback also helps people navigate the subtle dynamics of power and participation – understanding when to step forward and when to step back, as well as when they’re contributing productively and when they might be unintentionally dominating or holding back. Without such distributed feedback and coaching, collaborative approaches tend to break down, as people lack the information they need to adjust their behaviour and work better together.

A feedback culture normalises the giving and receiving of observations, reactions, and reflections as a natural part of collaboration. This requires developing specific skills: knowing how to offer feedback that is direct and clear, timely, and comes from a place of wanting to support the person’s growth rather than criticise or blame them. Equally important are skills in receiving feedback – listening to feedback with curiosity rather than defensiveness, and understanding the difference between evaluation and development.

Likewise, addressing conflict directly is critical in collective forms of leadership cultures, where the team must develop the capacity to work through disagreements, tensions, and differing perspectives together. This means learning to name tensions early – when they are still small and manageable – rather than allowing them to accumulate into larger conflicts that damage relationships and undermine trust. It also means distinguishing between different types of conflict: task-related disagreements that can strengthen decision-making versus interpersonal tensions that require relationship repair. Without having skills and processes for addressing conflict, teams default to avoidance, which leads to passive-aggressive behaviour, behind-the-scenes complaints, and the erosion of psychological safety.

Similarly, in collective forms of leadership cultures, coaching shifts from a specialised role to a shared skill. Team members develop the ability to ask powerful questions, listen deeply, and support one another’s growth and problem-solving. Such peer coaching recognises that expertise and wisdom exist throughout the organisation, not just in official leadership positions. For managers in hierarchical organisations, this represents a significant transition. They must shift from solving problems for their employees to supporting people in finding their own solutions through asking questions.

For deeper conflicts or relationship repair, different languages and processes are used. Tuff Leadership Training uses the phrase “moose heads on the table” to signify these bigger tensions that everyone senses but no one is addressing. They have defined processes for relationship repair conversations to work through more substantial conflicts.

The key practice here is developing a shared language and practices that allow your team to engage in different types of conversations.

Voices from the field

Karin Tenelius starts with a simple observation: self-organising requires more collaboration, collaboration requires communication, and communication requires a language for things that are usually left unnamed. “Between people, there are interpersonal relationships happening; that’s air, basically. But the air has qualities: moods, tension or not. In a team, there’s a working climate, and you need a completely different language to be able to name what’s going on between people.” Part of what she and her colleagues do when working with teams is help them develop that language.

The examples she gives are small but telling. The word pebble is used to name a friction between two people: something like a stone in your shoe that is tickling or hurting you, that, in most workplaces, would simply be left untouched. “In ordinary workplaces, that friction just goes on for ages; some people don’t talk to each other if they don’t like each other. But in self-organising teams, we have to cooperate, so we need to resolve it.” A moose head is something bigger: a tension that has become infected, perhaps taboo, that everyone in the room senses but nobody is addressing. These invented words do something important. They make it possible to talk about things that previously had no name and therefore no handle.

The core objective of the rituals Karin recommends is to keep the culture open: to maintain an environment where nothing is too small to address and where the working climate is often tended to. Quality check-ins, she stresses, are not about reporting what you did last weekend or announcing that you feel fine. They are about genuinely attending to how people are doing and how they are. Quarterly meetings include a dedicated moment to ask whether any moose heads have surfaced. Feedback is built in as an ongoing practice rather than reserved for formal reviews, with the team developing shared agreements about how to name and address behaviour that is getting in the way. “It’s really about keeping communication and the working climate clean.”

On the question of when and how feedback happens, Karin is pragmatic. Some of it is responsive: if someone is behaving in a way that is not helpful, there needs to be an agreement that a colleague can name it at the moment. Some of it is structural: evaluating after a meeting what worked and what did not, as a regular ritual, so that the team can keep refining how it operates together. Decision-making processes are themselves rituals, she argues, ensuring that mandates are kept where they belong and that before anyone makes a decision that affects others, there is a shared process for doing so. Importantly, she cautions against trying to design all of this in advance. “It’s better to carve out rituals and procedures along the way, as things occur that need to be addressed.”

When teams come from more hierarchical cultures where psychological safety is low, she does not begin by demanding openness. She begins by onboarding people into the idea of it: acknowledging the fear that is present, making it nameable, and letting openness grow from there. “Openness feeds openness,” she says. Most people, in her experience, actually want to be more open. What stands in the way is fear, and fear responds to being named. “Ask: what is it that we don’t talk about? And then openness is created.” On power, her approach is similarly direct. Rather than committing to flattening all hierarchies, she encourages teams to name the hierarchies that actually exist, including knowledge and competence hierarchies, and to talk about them honestly. A founder who holds power by virtue of being a founder needs to acknowledge that explicitly and take responsibility for how it shows up. “It’s resolved through communication,” she says.

3.5Talking about power and practising power differently#

A challenging cultural shift for collaborative leadership involves naming and changing power dynamics. Traditional organisations often avoid explicit discussion of power, preferring to maintain the false idea that hierarchies are purely functional or based solely on merit. Yet power – formal and informal, visible and invisible – shapes our interactions.

Collaborative leadership requires developing a collective understanding of power: recognising how it operates, acknowledging who holds it in different contexts, and discussing how it’s used. One way to understand and talk about power is to distinguish between four different types: power-over (domination and control); power-under (resistance); power-within (personal agency and self-determination); and power-with (collective capacity and working together as equals). While hierarchical organisations depend primarily on power-over, collaborative leadership aims to develop power-within and power-with.

This transformation is not about eliminating all hierarchy or pretending everyone has equal power. Rather, it involves being explicit about where formal authority resides, having clarity about where decisions are made and who holds accountability for them, and ensuring that those most affected by decisions have a meaningful voice in making them. It means recognising when hierarchical decision-making is appropriate and when a more collective form of leadership serves the organisation better.

Practice

A practice for exploring different forms of power#

Review the model on power (Starhawk, 1987). Individually, reflect on examples for each type of power – how does each one manifest in your life and work?

How do different forms of power feel?

An embodied practice can deepen this reflection: write each power type on a piece of paper or sticky note and place them on the floor. Stand on each paper in turn – how does it feel to stand in each type of power? This embodied layer can add another dimension, helping you recognise how these powers feel in your body and how to recognise them in practice.

After individual exploration, pair up with a colleague and share your examples. This provides a more intimate space for sharing than a large group setting and can be a good way to deepen your understanding of each other.

Finally, come together as a whole group to discuss: How could the team support “power with” and “power within”? What specific practices or changes would strengthen these forms of power?

Voices from the field

Transforming culture in organisations that have long operated as a hierarchy is rarely a question of implementing the right framework or attending the right training. Nati Lombardo, who has worked with NGOs and cooperatives through such transitions, usually starts with something simpler.

“When it comes down to it, our main advice is: talk about it. Just talk about all the things you think you can talk about. Make things explicit. Make space for people to be able to name what they need, what they want, what they’re scared of.” The phrase “bringing your whole self to work” may have become a cliché, she acknowledges, but the underlying idea is genuine. Creating space for people to show up more fully is not a nice addition to cultural change. It is the beginning of it. And that space cannot be designed from above and handed down. It has to be built together, starting from real questions: what does each person in this team need? What do we all bring? What happens when my personality meets yours? “From that place,” she says, “you can decide together what kind of culture you want to have, and then ask: where is the gap between where we are now and where we want to go, and how do we support each other?”

One of the conversations that tends to be hardest to open, particularly in Scandinavian contexts where the word itself carries uncomfortable associations, is the conversation about power. Nati is direct about what makes it possible. “It’s quite important that the people who already hold some of the power, whether social capital or explicit role-based power, are the ones who open up the conversation.” An external facilitator can help create conditions for this, but the key move is modelling: showing, through how you show up, that talking about power is not an accusation or a threat but a necessary part of building something more honest together. Nati recalls a fishbowl exercise in one of her workshops where several participants found it genuinely difficult to acknowledge the power they held, precisely because they associated power with something negative. What changes when more nuance enters that conversation – when power can be talked about in terms of how it is earned, how it can be used well, and how it can be distributed – is that the topic stops being taboo and starts being workable in the team.

That same dynamic, she argues, is at the heart of the most common obstacle she encounters when working with teams on cultural change. You can put the right structures in place, agree on the right values, and run the right workshops, but if the people who hold formal or informal power behave in ways that contradict what is being said, the culture will not shift. “You can talk about a lot of things, but if the person who holds that leadership role shows up in a way that contradicts all of their stated ideals, the culture is really not going to shift, and it will be much more difficult.” Role modeling, she says, is not one ingredient among many. It is the condition that determines whether everything else lands or not.

3.6Challenges with distributed teams#

On a final note, developing a culture for leading together can be challenging – especially as remote and distributed work styles become more common. Online work and geographic separation makes the informal relationship-building that supports trust and psychological safety more difficult due to things like having a more task-based focus and a lack of spontaneous interactions. To navigate these challenges, we recommend experimenting with structures that support teams in building a culture of safety, and that help deepen relationships. This demands prioritising bringing people together for relationship-building, creating explicit agreements about how teams will work together, and continually examining whether the culture and relationships are supported with the actual practices.

Practice

A practice for developing team agreement#

With your team, discuss your shared purpose and values, and identify which behaviours support these. In the previous chapter on self-leadership, we introduced the Personal Canvas practice. Look at that practice and build upon it, progressing from individual reflections to reflections on team level.

After each team member has created a personal canvas with their own reflections about purpose, values, passions and desired impact, invite everyone to share their thoughts and ask the team to distill core shared values on the team level. How can a team formulate a shared purpose that bridges the individual sense of purpose with a purpose that everyone on the team shares, while also being connected to the overall purpose of organisation? Invite team members to also reflect on what kind of supporting behaviours would be needed on the team to enable them to enact shared purpose in everyday work. Then come together as a whole group to discuss and find a shared understanding of supporting behaviours and how they can be transformed into concrete work principles and activities integrated into the team’s daily work.

You can use sticky notes to capture and cluster ideas, or create a visual collage such as a tree – with roots representing your values, the trunk representing your purpose, and leaves representing the behaviours and activities that bring these to life.

Developing shared values, purpose and desired team activities and behaviours.

Revisit these agreements regularly to ensure they continue to support how you actually work together. You can hang a shared team agreement or manifesto in the team’s shared work space as a daily reminder and to make it more accessible as something you can always easily come back to.

For a structured template to guide a team agreement process, we recommend using The Hum’s How do we want to be together? exercise which can be found at thehum.org/visitors/resources. (It’s free. Create a free account to access it). This exercise provides a framework for creating team agreements that support your collaborative culture.

Voices from the field

The practitioners we spoke to have each developed their own answer to the question of what cultural change actually looks like in practice, not as a set of abstract principles but as repeatable ways of being together that teams can build into their daily work.

Mette Aagaard draws heavily on the sociocracy toolbox*, a set of principles and structures she has seen work across very different organisations. “Personally, I find it hard to take part in meetings or dialogue that don’t adhere to these kinds of principles,” she says. The practices are specific and unglamorous, but that is precisely the point.

It starts with the check-in: not a 30 update on what everyone has been doing, but a brief moment to ensure that each person is genuinely present and that nothing is standing in the way of their full attention. Then there is decision-making by consent, which she is an enthusiastic advocate for because it sidesteps the power struggles that tend to emerge from consensus-based processes. Speaking in rounds ensures that talking time is distributed rather than captured by the most confident voices. Having a dedicated facilitator for each meeting means that when something difficult needs to be brought to the table, whether because the content is complex or because emotions are running high, there is someone whose explicit role is to hold the space. “It’s about helping the team refrain from moving into decision-making mode when what we are discussing is not yet ready for a decision, but needs more exploration of perspectives in order to better understand the complexity of an issue.”

Working with roles and how they are distributed and nominated among colleagues is another piece, one she acknowledges can be genuinely sensitive. And then there is transparency, which she finds teams often struggle with in unexpected ways. She describes one team that considered introducing full transparency around the registration of working hours. The idea came from a place of mutual care: without a manager surveying hours, colleagues had no way of knowing if someone was accumulating far too many and needed help or protection. But others experienced the proposal as surveillance. “I don’t want my colleagues to monitor when or how much I’m working, and I feel it becomes a competition for who’s accumulated the most hours, as in who’s the best colleague.” The conversation took a long time and carried a lot of hard feelings. Eventually, they went ahead with it. And what happened was not competition or blaming each other: they discovered that one colleague had been registering fewer hours than she should have been.When they asked what was going on, it turned out she was navigating a difficult situation at home involving a child who was struggling to attend school. “And then the team said: now that we know this, how can we help you?” The transparency that had felt threatening became the condition for support.

Nati Lombardo offers two stories from her own experience that, together, capture something important about what makes this work succeed and what can make it fall apart even when everything seems to be in place.

* sociocracyforall.org/and Sociocracy handbook “Many Voices One Song”: sociocracyforall.org/many-voices-one-song/

The first is the case of Loomio, the organisation where she first began working in this way. By most measures, the conditions were right. The culture was strong, the processes were well designed, and the team had even built their own technology to support their way of working. “We had great processes. We had the technology. We built it ourselves to enable this way of working.” And yet it became unsustainable, not because of anything internal to the culture or the organisation, but because the funding was not there. The income was not being generated. “So it’s like: we can look at all the pieces, but sometimes it’s not just about the culture and how we organise. It’s also about what’s happening in the world. What is the context? How do we pay ourselves?” The lesson Nati draws from Loomio is not a counsel of despair but a reminder that cultural and organisational transformation does not happen in a vacuum. External factors matter, and sometimes, despite everything, you have to let go.

The second story is more hopeful. Working with a cooperative in the UK, Nati encountered a team that had already been on their journey for five or six years by the time she came in to support their decision-making processes. What struck her was not the absence of challenges but the quality of the response to them: a genuine collective willingness to keep improving, keep changing, keep trying rather than giving up. “Everyone was really engaged in what was happening.” The team had a high level of trust, and had built real relationships with each other despite being geographically distributed. They also brought something to their difficulties that cannot be installed through a workshop or framework: the disposition to stay in it together. That, she suggests, is what the culture ultimately needs to make possible.

3.7Questions for reflection#

  1. What are your team’s shared core values and purpose?

  2. How are they connected to the overall values and purpose of your organisation?

  3. What kinds of behaviours are crucial for enacting your team’s shared values and purpose in your daily work?

  4. How do you experience psychological safety in your team?

  5. What small, concrete actions can your team take to strengthen psychological safety?

  6. What is the most common way of listening in your team (downloading, factual, empathic, or generative)?

  7. How can you integrate intentional practice of more empathic and generative listening and dialogue into your daily work and into your team rituals?

  8. How is coaching and the giving and receiving of feedback practised in your team/organisation today?

  9. Does responsibility lie with the manager(s) or is it shared among team colleagues?

  10. How can you develop and integrate peer-to-peer feedback and coaching practices into your team/organisational culture?

  11. How do you deal with conflicts in your team/organisation?

  12. What would it take to build a culture in which you can openly talk about conflict together?

  13. How is power exercised in your team/organisation (formally and informally)?

  14. Can you talk openly about power in your team/organisation? If not, what would need to change to make that possible?

  15. Which forms of power (power-over, power-under, power-within and power-with) would you say are most often exercised in your team/ organisation? In what situations?

  16. How can you strengthen your team/organisation to practise more power-within and power-with?

3.8Conclusions#

In this chapter, we introduced different aspects of building a culture for leading together – from psychological safety to deeper levels of generative listening and dialogue. Feedback, coaching, conflict resolution, and distributing power have been discussed as cornerstones for sharing leadership. We argued that creating a culture where team members feel safe to be themselves and experience trust between each other – allowing them to take risks to both try out new things together and bring tough questions to the table – is a crucial condition for developing shared leadership. Teams need to openly discuss and reach a consensus about their shared values and purpose, as well as ways of working together that can support them in enacting their values and following their “why” through daily work routines. Too often, values and purpose statements remain glossy words published on corporate websites but are not felt and embodied in workplaces through people’s behaviours.

Building a culture of shared leadership is not easy. Especially in organisations with a long tradition of hierarchies and in a fast-paced, digitalised society where remote working is increasingly common and closing ourselves off in our own digital bubbles is fast becoming the norm. Building strong team rituals that cultivate deep relationships as well as support between team members and collective learning has become harder and is, nowadays, rather an exception than the norm. Such rituals demand a conscious effort that goes beyond yearly get-together meetings, and is sustained over time.

Formal managers often play an important role when a culture of shared leadership is being built, especially in the first phase as the organisation or team transitions away from traditional hierarchies. They must dare to break away from established patterns and norms and model another type of leadership, being courageous enough to open up, be vulnerable, and talk about their own challenges in the transformation journey. Managers need to actively let go of control and invite other colleagues to take up more space, assuming a more facilitative approach to leadership. This entails shifting from a parent-child dynamic in their relationship with colleagues towards an adult-adult dynamic. Sharing power and responsibility is not easy for managers who have built their whole career in a hierarchical setup.

What can help managers is adopting a practice of continuously inviting other colleagues into co-creation processes and facilitating them in a way that gives all team members the time and space they need to contribute. As part of these processes, the team can discuss and agree upon shared ways of working together, and establish regular team rituals. Team rituals should support the deepening of relationships and trust in the team while opening a forum for talks about challenges and conflicts, retrospectives on what’s going well and where there is space for development, as well as for discussing challenging topics like power. Holding space for team members to practise giving and receiving feedback and coaching each other is an important part of team rituals for shared leadership. Especially in the beginning, when team members are not well-versed in these practices, dedicating time and space for developing and practising feedback and coaching skills is necessary before they can be smoothly integrated into daily ways of working.
