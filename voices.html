Voices from the field

The leaders and consultants we spoke to during our research were consistent on one point: the case for change no longer needs to be argued. The people inside public organisations already feel it.

Mette Aagaard has spent years working with public organisations on distributed leadership. When we asked her whether the case for change still needs to be made, her answer was almost impatient. “When I meet leaders from the public sector, they feel the pain. They don’t need me to explain why it’s needed.” What she sees, again and again, is the same impossible equation: the demands placed on public organisations, the resources available, and what citizens expect simply do not add up. And when organisations feel that pressure, they reach for the only lever they know. “Historically we have just been turning the optimisation dial like crazy. In the lack of imagination, we can’t really see any other button to push. And it doesn’t solve the problem.”

The greater difficulty, she argues, is about imagination. It is genuinely hard for people to picture a different way of organising, one that moves beyond the binary of either “manager decides” or full consensus, or that replaces control procedures and cascading targets with something more human. As an example of how far this has gone, she mentions that each Danish public school must meet an estimated 3,000-4,000 targets set for them by both local and national governments – and sometimes even parents! “It’s not humanly possible!”

Karin Tenelius builds on this with a simple insight: the real source of organisational efficiency is not process or structure. It is engagement. “The big ‘why’ is really effectiveness, which comes from unleashing engagement and accountability in many people. Accountability comes from engagement. That’s the big game.” The evidence, she says, is visible in organisations that have made the shift. Sick leave goes down. Staff turnover falls. Financial performance improves, sometimes even when salaries are raised at the same time – this happened with some of the companies she acquired, and where self-organising principles were introduced.

The same logic holds in the public sector, where the bottom line is measured differently. However, it shows up in different places. Flatter structures mean fewer unnecessary management layers. Response times to citizens improve. And something harder to measure but equally significant shifts: the organisation starts to be seen as genuinely oriented towards the people it serves. “People can see the organisation is there for the citizens, not for itself. They have a bigger picture!” The benefits, she argues, are the same as anywhere else. Only the metrics are different.

Chapter 1 · Cracks in our society and working environments

Voice from the artistic field

The cracks in society are, in a deeper sense, problems of imagination. We have built systems that reflect a particular understanding of what human beings are, what they are capable of, and what they are for. Changing those systems requires a different way of seeing. That is where art enters.

When asked about it, Dejan Srhoj sits for a while to reflect from his artistic perspective, considering how such cracks relate to leading together. One of the most consequential shifts in performing arts in the 20th century happened in the New York dance scene of the 1960s, with the introduction of what practitioners called scores. A score, as Dejan describes it, is a set of proposals, rules, indications or prompts that allow a choreography to unfold. When the score arrived, it replaced the authority of the choreographer. “Suddenly, you do not refer to a person anymore who knows, or who is the one that decides success or not. You refer to a score.” And in that shift, many new possibilities opened up that had not been possible before: events, relations, outputs that nobody could have predicted. All of it emerged as the score was practised. Failure became allowable. Not knowing became allowable. Being surprised became allowable.

The effect on dance and choreographic forms was enormous. Groups of people could practise a score together that might last one minute or two years. The research process became democratic. And perhaps most importantly, each participant was invited to contextualise what was happening from inside their own experience of the practice, rather than from the outside position of someone being directed. “To me, it feels that through that you allow each participant to contextualise as the person is doing the practice.”

Dejan also reflects on the risks of translating this kind of artistic practice into organisational contexts. The danger is commodification: someone takes the form of the score and puts it to work in the service of the same productivity logic it was designed to resist. But the score, he argues, tends to resist this precisely because it operates through intention. How you are within the practice shapes everything that follows: the practice itself, what emerges from it, what it means. “With the intention, then everything changes.” What it requires, in an organisational setting, is courage from leadership to allow practices whose outcomes cannot be specified in advance – a quality that is genuinely difficult to manufacture.

This difficulty highlights one of the most telling differences in how art and most workplaces understand human beings. In the performing arts, practitioners spend most of their time rehearsing – practising, exploring, failing, trying again – in order to perform occasionally when there is an audience. In most workplaces, that ratio is reversed. Every hour is expected to produce specific outputs. Every meeting is supposed to arrive somewhere. The space for exploration, for the kind of tuning in that precedes understanding, has been compressed to almost nothing. However, as Dejan observes, such tuning in is not a luxury. It is what we need to spend most of our time and attention on, whether we acknowledge it or not. “We know from everyday life that the majority of time is either tuning into something, tuning out of something, trying to understand, trying to fit in, finding ways to relate. We need a lot of time to understand where we are in space, where we are in time and how we relate to people who are there.”

For Dejan, this is at its roots a question of trust, a question that sits at the heart of the shift towards leading together. “Do we trust that our employees are there in order to create value for the organisation but also for themselves?” When the answer is uncertain, organisations reach for control, and control demands that everyone at every level is performing for the person above them, demonstrating that what they are doing makes sense. But that performance consumes the very time and attention that work requires. Refusing to acknowledge this perspective, according to Dejan, means we have created a system that expects something from people that they cannot actually deliver.

He pauses a minute to laugh about the ironies: as a performer, Dejan is expected to perform far less than most people in organisations. When he performs, everyone knows it is happening. The rest of the time, he is practising. “It’s very funny that I, as a performer, need to perform less than you do in your organisation, but I’m called a performer.”

The system that demands constant performance not only exhausts people. It traps them in a loop that is difficult to see from the inside. You report to a manager, the manager reports to another manager, all the way up to a director, who is accountable to a politician, who is accountable to a prime minister, who is accountable to voters. And voters, which is to say all of us, demand clear answers: what was the money spent on, how was it spent? We do not want too much experimentation. We just want things to work. “So in a way, it’s us who are unhappy with the system, but it’s also us voting for politicians who will deliver in a way what we don’t want.” We want things to work instantaneously, and we also want the freedom to explore, experiment and improvise. The system cannot give us both, and we keep asking it to.

That is why, Dejan says, he keeps returning to the organisational level, to the everyday work environment, to the small scale where change can start. It is hard to expect entire countries to shift the paradigm. But teams and organisations can. And if enough of them do, it might create a movement on higher levels. Dejan has experienced this tension firsthand: as part of a European Union project, he has been expected to fill in forms accounting for his working hours. He resists it. “I think I will just have to fake it somehow. How can I account for hours of thinking, preparing, talking, observing, dancing, and doing nothing, which all form a process that enables me to create? I need to keep the dignity and the trust that I’m doing something that has value in my own way. Otherwise I can start to doubt myself: am I doing it right, am I doing it enough, should it be more, should it be less?” The form does not measure what he is doing. It measures his compliance with a system that needs to believe it is measuring something.

This is one of the most dangerous effects of the current paradigm. And it connects directly to the internal cracks described earlier in this chapter. Organisations are not interested in the whole person. They are interested in one specific aspect: the rational mind, the flexible and quick response, the deliverable. Everything else, the body, the emotions, the relational self, the parts of a person that need time to tune in and find meaning, is left outside the door. “We are expected to engage at the workplace only partially.” And that accumulates. In the short and medium term, that partial engagement creates stress, burnout, and mental health problems. Over time, it prevents people from developing the capacities they do not even know they have, because those capacities only emerge through relationships, through being met as a whole person rather than as a function. “We do not exist on our own, but we exist as relational beings to other humans, living beings and objects. And if those relations are continually partial, then we stay partial.”

We believe that Dejan’s views open up a necessary dialogue on the transformation this book is about: if the current ways of constant control and performance are not only exhausting but structurally incapable of producing what we need, then what would it mean to build organisations around a different understanding of how humans work, create and lead together?

Voice from the artistic field

For at least a hundred years, avant-garde and contemporary art have been working with the same problems that organisations are now becoming more interested in exploring. How do people share authority? How does something genuine emerge from a group? What happens to time, space, and perception when you stop organising them from the top down? Dejan Srhoj, who comes from the world of contemporary performing arts, has spent years thinking about what that accumulated artistic knowledge might offer organisations trying to carry out a transformation towards collective forms of leadership.

“Contemporary art has been experimenting heavily with how people are together,” he says. “How do we understand or play with time, how do we play with space, what are the ways of being together, mixing everyday life and art, understanding intimacy as a political act?” This includes what I feel, how I relate to others, how I use everyday situations, how I show up in a room with other people. These intimate aspects constitute the substance of how we organise ourselves.

Much of this knowledge, he observes, developed in isolated pockets: avant-garde art forms in the 1920s, a particular approach to choreography in the 1960s, “conceptual dance” in the 1990s – different experiments that took place in specific spaces and timeframes and never quite escaped them. But the knowledge is there, rich and largely unexplored, available to be reapproached not only within the art field but from anywhere where people are trying to change how they work together. What gives him hope is that by translating this knowledge into organisational contexts, the knowledge might reach the scale it was aiming for: “Some of the wishes and ideas that artists had might actually be practised by many more people, especially by people or organisations that have perhaps more power in the process of social change.”

Chapter 1 · Why artistic methods support transformation

Voices from the field

Change often shows up in small moments: a meeting that finally felt worth attending, a colleague whose voice you had never properly heard, a task you never thought you would be trusted with. The experts we spoke to collect these moments carefully, because they know that in the middle of a difficult transformation, small stories of impact matter just as much as large ones.

Mette Aagaard is candid about the nature of her work: she is usually called in when things get hard, which means her archive of stories leans more towards struggle than triumph. But even within that, she notices something accumulating. One story that stays with her is of an organisation that managed to shift its strategic direction without the usual disruption of restructuring reporting lines, a small but significant sign of what becomes possible when decision-making is genuinely distributed. More often, though, what she carries are quieter moments. The time a team member shared that it had become easier to focus once interpersonal dynamics stopped consuming so much energy: “I can actually focus on the job I need to get done.” A nurse who had spent her entire career being told she needed to listen better, without ever quite managing it, until a change in how her team structured its dialogues forced a different kind of attention. “I feel like I have new colleagues now,” the nurse told her. “These three colleagues who I’ve never really heard from because they’re quieter people, they’re extremely clever and they come up with ideas I couldn’t have come up with.” And a team member who, simply because the new structure allowed people to nominate themselves for tasks, found herself working outside her usual professional domain for the first time. “It’s more like little stories of impact rather than one big story,” Mette says. “But they add up.”

Nati Lombardo came to this work through a worker-owned software cooperative before turning her attention to NGOs. A background that sharpened her eye for a particular kind of organisational contradiction. The organisations she began working with held genuinely progressive values about how they wanted to show up in the world. Yet internally, they were still running on structures that didn’t reflect any of that. Drawing upon Laloux’s framework, she describes it as organisations holding green values but living in an orange environment. “They started to notice that internally they were still upholding very old structures that were not serving them. And they were like: how do we change?”

What shifts once those organisations begin to align how they operate with what they stand for is, according to Nati, primarily a sense of empowerment: the realisation that it is possible to live your values inside the organisation, not only in what it does externally. That said, she is careful not to overstate the progress. Funding constraints in the NGO world limit how much flexibility organisations can realistically afford, and many teams are still somewhere in the middle of their journey. A common sticking point is the move beyond the initial opening up, the moment of full consensus and flat equality, towards something more workable and sustainable. “How can we be more efficient while still holding those values?” That, she says, is where the real and ongoing work lies.

Chapter 2 · What is self-leadership and why it is important for leading together

Voices from the field

Self-leadership is sometimes spoken about as though it were simply a matter of individual discipline. But the practitioners we spoke to view it as something fundamentally more relational: how we know ourselves shapes how we show up with others. That connection between the inner and the collective is where much of the real work of transformation happens.

Karin Tenelius points to something she has observed repeatedly in her work with self-organising teams: the process of working differently together does not just change how decisions get made. It changes people. “I think it’s an even stronger thing than awareness,” she says. “It’s self-insight. And it’s fascinating how much self-insight self-organising processes generate in people.” She recalls working with a call centre many years ago, with a team of down-to-earth, practical people who would not necessarily have described themselves as particularly reflective. Over the course of two, three or four years, something unexpected emerged: people made significant life decisions, pursued university degrees they had never previously considered, and stepped into professional roles that had seemed out of reach. One former employee went on to become a senior manager in a municipality, something that, as Karin puts it, “was not on the cards for her.” Self-insight, she argues, is the foundation from which self-regulation and self-leadership actually grow, and it is the self-organising process itself that tends to generate it. In our research processes, that self-insight sometimes resulted in participants realising they were not in the right workplace and changing jobs. In other cases, participants realised that, throughout their lives, they had abandoned doing things that were an important source of joy, and ended up returning to them over the course of the project.

Mette Aagaard recognises the same dynamic but approaches it from a slightly different angle. The transformation she describes requires awareness at a very practical level: becoming conscious of the habitual patterns we have built up over years of working within hierarchical structures. An employee who has always delegated upward, always expected the manager to take responsibility, and has found it quite comfortable to complain when problems are not solved from above, needs to become aware of that pattern before anything can shift. “It demands more from both managers and employees to be aware of their natural patterns, and then to proactively direct themselves to change that pattern. And then to self-regulate when they notice they’ve slipped back into the old ways.”

That said, Mette is candid about her own ambivalence when it comes to the question of where to start. Her primary focus is on getting teams to work, and for her, the relationship between individual self-leadership and team dynamics is more like a chicken and egg than a clear sequence. Rather than beginning with the individual and telling them they need to manage themselves better, she prefers to change the team dynamic first and trust that self-insight will follow. “I would support people’s self-insight through deploying a different dynamic in the team, rather than starting with the individual.” What she has seen get in the way most often is not a lack of willingness but a lack of awareness: individuals who push their preferences too hard without noticing the effect on others, who are too confident that their way is simply the right way, and who interpret difference as a failure of intelligence rather than a different perspective. “I completely agree that self-leadership is important. It’s just that I prefer to support individuals’ ability to interact with others while working with that in a team setting. My focus is not so much on self-management of the individual in isolation.”

This is something we have also learned in our own research. Many organisations in Sweden approached us after the COVID-19 pandemic, asking us to support their efforts to strengthen self-leadership among their employees. However, focusing too much, or solely, on strengthening individuals’ ability to manage themselves often increases pressure and expectations around individual performance. This, in turn, increases stress levels and the risk of burnout. Most importantly, it weakens the collective focus on shared purpose, mutual care, support and collective learning, which is the cornerstone of building sustainable, meaningful, effective and innovative organisations.

Chapter 2 · What is self-leadership and why it is important for leading together

Voices from the field

Moving towards self-organising ways of working places a real and sometimes underestimated demand on people, particularly those who are at an earlier stage of their careers or who have never worked in environments where taking initiative was genuinely encouraged or safe. Nati Lombardo has observed this tension closely in her work with NGOs and cooperative organisations.

“People who might be quite young when they enter the workforce don’t yet have that built in,” she says. “It takes time and a certain maturity to develop self-leadership.” The problem is often compounded by the managers around them, who are more accustomed to delegating tasks and directing people than to supporting them in growing into their own leadership space. The result can be a kind of paradox: organisations that open up space for self-leadership without building the conditions for people to actually grow into it. “We are expecting people to be able to do it, but if they can’t, we don’t know how to help them get there.”

Her answer highlights a shift in how managers understand their role. Rather than focusing on task delegation, coaching is the more useful orientation, where the manager understands what a particular person needs in order to step into their own leadership and creates the conditions for that to happen. But crucially, she argues, that responsibility should not solely rest with the manager. Distributing the role of supporting others across the whole team is itself a form of empowerment. “It’s like distributed empowerment,” she says. “I think that’s quite important.”

She also pushes back on an assumption she often hears from managers: that people simply prefer to be told what to do. In her experience, that preference is rarely as fixed as it appears. “Is it that they don’t want it, or is it that they never had the experience or a context where they could do that, and therefore they don’t know how?” The distinction matters enormously – one calls for acceptance and the other calls for intentional design. And the way forward, she suggests, is less about individual coaching in isolation and more about creating spaces where teams can openly and explicitly talk about what they are trying to build together, what support is needed, where they still fall short, and what skills they need to develop collectively. “It takes time,” she says. “Not just the manager with new people, but for everyone.”

Chapter 2 · In what ways can self-leadership stand in the way of leading together?

Voice from the artistic field

When an organisation decides to initiate a process of transformation using artistic methods, something begins to unfold that is difficult to understand from the outside. Dejan Srhoj describes it as a space of research: one that requires a certain courage from leadership to enter, because the outcomes cannot be predetermined. As the creative process of transformation develops, he says, you encounter challenges and problems, but also joys and desires. What he and his colleagues do as facilitators, within that space, is invite people to question their own role, not only within the organisation, but as individuals in relation to others.

The vehicle for such questioning is often movement. Through dance and physical exercises, he invites people to try things they would not normally do: to lead when they are habitual followers, to follow when they are usually leaders. The experience opens up questions: Do I feel that everything will collapse if I do not lead? If I take action, will I destroy what is already happening, as if there were a plan my initiative might ruin? These are psychological questions that the exercises make visible, giving people a direct physical experience with their own patterns in a context where there is no right way to be. “If our task is to research, if our task is experimentation, if our task is innovation, then through those workshops we invite people to take action, to take responsibility for their own proposals, in a context that does not judge what they have proposed, which allows for unexpected outputs and which allows for failure as well.” For Dejan, this is what self-leadership looks like from the inside: not a set of skills to be acquired, but an awareness and a self-emancipation within the body, within oneself, and then in relation to the group and the organisation.

What makes the body and movement essential to this process, rather than simply one method among many, is a question Dejan has thought about. The answer begins with time.

Through physical exercise, the experience of leading or following, of feeling safe or not safe, of knowing or not knowing, is immediate. There is no discussion about it first. The experience itself gives you the information directly, and reflection arrives afterward to place it in the context. “I believe that movement and dance have their own logic, their own language, their own ways of understanding, which are many times faster than through language and thinking with our brain. It’s as if we unlock something.” That unlocking, he argues, is not metaphorical. As human beings, we have understood ourselves and related to each other through doing, playing, working and moving together for millions of years. The capacity to navigate a room full of other people, to sense who is leading, who is following, who has a particular energy that makes you want to move with them, has been practised across our history as humans. Sitting in meetings, using computers, resolving issues through language alone: that is very recent. “Maybe evolution will get us there, maybe in 50,000 years we will be able to resolve things only by sitting, but right now we are not there at all.” What embodied practice does is it gives us access to a deeper layer of knowing. A knowing we have built across generations and can no longer consciously remember, but that our bodies still hold. He offers a simple example that illustrates this. An exercise he often returns to is one where a group simply walks together. When one person stops, everyone stops. When one person starts to walk, everyone starts to walk. Nothing more than that. And yet, within half an hour, that exercise yields knowledge that would be extremely difficult and time-consuming to arrive at through conversation alone: who the people in the room are, how they behave, who creates a genuine bodily pull to follow certain people and who follows only because the exercise requires it, who leads because they carry a particular energy and who leads because they have decided they should. That speed – and that directness of access – is what embodied practices bring to the work of leading together.

Chapter 2 · In what ways can self-leadership stand in the way of leading together?

Voice from the artistic field

In our research projects, we employed artistic practices to help build psychological safety among participants. Choreographer Dejan Srhoj reflects on how a choreographic practice that emphasises investing time to tune in before people start working together – by slowing down and bringing awareness to space, time, relationships, and feelings – strengthens different aspects of psychological safety, including trust, taking risks, inclusion and open conversations:

“Before work happens in a room, something else has to happen first. Where am I physically? What is this space? What is its temperature?How large is it? Do I feel safe here? Do I feel welcome? Who are the people around me, and how do I relate to them? Where did I come from before this meeting, and where am I going afterward? These are not trivial questions to be ignored to start the ’real’ work. They are the real work, or at least the necessary ground for it. And they take time.”

In dance and choreography, Dejan says this tuning is so fundamental that practitioners do it without thinking. It is the default, the starting point of every rehearsal, something nobody questions. In his work with organisations, however, he discovered that this is far from the default elsewhere. “People simply sit and start talking, as if you can jump over all of this just because you told yourself,: let’s just start working. But it doesn’t work like that.” The body is still in transit, still arriving, still negotiating the room and the people in it, while the meeting has already begun.

What choreography brings, then, is an understanding of dramaturgy: the thinking about how an event is structured in time and space. When do people move, when do they reflect, and when do they rest? How is the space organised? How much time is devoted to each phase, and in what sequence? “Everything is important: when you have the break, what kind of break it is, when you reflect, how you reflect, when you move and for how long.” This is nothing to be stressed about, but rather, something to be attentive to. And the answers might differ from time to time, from place to place. Recognising how people inhabit a shared space and time shapes everything that becomes possible within it.

The effect of understanding and attending to this, Dejan says, is trust. Not trust as an abstract value to be stated or agreed upon, but trust as in something that actually arises in a body, in a room, and between people. And from trust arises something else that organisations rarely make room for: playfulness. “We’ve established trust, so we can be playful. And within that playfulness, different qualities of people can emerge, some of them that we know and some of them that are not known.” Playfulness allows for silliness, for not knowing, for failure. It also creates something that is still rare in organisational life: a more equal starting point. When nobody already knows how to do the task at hand, when everyone is practising something new together, the hierarchy slowly dissipates.

What this setup ultimately makes possible, and what Dejan holds as a deep intention in this work, is the possibility of a whole person being present. Not the rational or the professional role alone, but the whole individual, the body, the emotions, the difficulties and desires. All the parts of a person that workplaces usually ask to be left outside the door. The embodied experience is meant to be brought into dialogue with the cognitive: how does what has just emerged in me translate into my everyday work and life? That translation, Dejan acknowledges, is where the biggest challenge lies. Some of it happens consciously, through reflection. And some of it, perhaps the biggest part, happens through continuous practice and without quite knowing how.

Chapter 3 · Creating psychological safety: The foundation for leading together

Voices from the field

Karin Tenelius starts with a simple observation: self-organising requires more collaboration, collaboration requires communication, and communication requires a language for things that are usually left unnamed. “Between people, there are interpersonal relationships happening; that’s air, basically. But the air has qualities: moods, tension or not. In a team, there’s a working climate, and you need a completely different language to be able to name what’s going on between people.” Part of what she and her colleagues do when working with teams is help them develop that language.

The examples she gives are small but telling. The word pebble is used to name a friction between two people: something like a stone in your shoe that is tickling or hurting you, that, in most workplaces, would simply be left untouched. “In ordinary workplaces, that friction just goes on for ages; some people don’t talk to each other if they don’t like each other. But in self-organising teams, we have to cooperate, so we need to resolve it.” A moose head is something bigger: a tension that has become infected, perhaps taboo, that everyone in the room senses but nobody is addressing. These invented words do something important. They make it possible to talk about things that previously had no name and therefore no handle.

The core objective of the rituals Karin recommends is to keep the culture open: to maintain an environment where nothing is too small to address and where the working climate is often tended to. Quality check-ins, she stresses, are not about reporting what you did last weekend or announcing that you feel fine. They are about genuinely attending to how people are doing and how they are. Quarterly meetings include a dedicated moment to ask whether any moose heads have surfaced. Feedback is built in as an ongoing practice rather than reserved for formal reviews, with the team developing shared agreements about how to name and address behaviour that is getting in the way. “It’s really about keeping communication and the working climate clean.”

On the question of when and how feedback happens, Karin is pragmatic. Some of it is responsive: if someone is behaving in a way that is not helpful, there needs to be an agreement that a colleague can name it at the moment. Some of it is structural: evaluating after a meeting what worked and what did not, as a regular ritual, so that the team can keep refining how it operates together. Decision-making processes are themselves rituals, she argues, ensuring that mandates are kept where they belong and that before anyone makes a decision that affects others, there is a shared process for doing so. Importantly, she cautions against trying to design all of this in advance. “It’s better to carve out rituals and procedures along the way, as things occur that need to be addressed.”

When teams come from more hierarchical cultures where psychological safety is low, she does not begin by demanding openness. She begins by onboarding people into the idea of it: acknowledging the fear that is present, making it nameable, and letting openness grow from there. “Openness feeds openness,” she says. Most people, in her experience, actually want to be more open. What stands in the way is fear, and fear responds to being named. “Ask: what is it that we don’t talk about? And then openness is created.” On power, her approach is similarly direct. Rather than committing to flattening all hierarchies, she encourages teams to name the hierarchies that actually exist, including knowledge and competence hierarchies, and to talk about them honestly. A founder who holds power by virtue of being a founder needs to acknowledge that explicitly and take responsibility for how it shows up. “It’s resolved through communication,” she says.

Chapter 3 · Building a culture of feedback, coaching and conflict resolution

Voices from the field

Transforming culture in organisations that have long operated as a hierarchy is rarely a question of implementing the right framework or attending the right training. Nati Lombardo, who has worked with NGOs and cooperatives through such transitions, usually starts with something simpler.

“When it comes down to it, our main advice is: talk about it. Just talk about all the things you think you can talk about. Make things explicit. Make space for people to be able to name what they need, what they want, what they’re scared of.” The phrase “bringing your whole self to work” may have become a cliché, she acknowledges, but the underlying idea is genuine. Creating space for people to show up more fully is not a nice addition to cultural change. It is the beginning of it. And that space cannot be designed from above and handed down. It has to be built together, starting from real questions: what does each person in this team need? What do we all bring? What happens when my personality meets yours? “From that place,” she says, “you can decide together what kind of culture you want to have, and then ask: where is the gap between where we are now and where we want to go, and how do we support each other?”

One of the conversations that tends to be hardest to open, particularly in Scandinavian contexts where the word itself carries uncomfortable associations, is the conversation about power. Nati is direct about what makes it possible. “It’s quite important that the people who already hold some of the power, whether social capital or explicit role-based power, are the ones who open up the conversation.” An external facilitator can help create conditions for this, but the key move is modelling: showing, through how you show up, that talking about power is not an accusation or a threat but a necessary part of building something more honest together. Nati recalls a fishbowl exercise in one of her workshops where several participants found it genuinely difficult to acknowledge the power they held, precisely because they associated power with something negative. What changes when more nuance enters that conversation – when power can be talked about in terms of how it is earned, how it can be used well, and how it can be distributed – is that the topic stops being taboo and starts being workable in the team.

That same dynamic, she argues, is at the heart of the most common obstacle she encounters when working with teams on cultural change. You can put the right structures in place, agree on the right values, and run the right workshops, but if the people who hold formal or informal power behave in ways that contradict what is being said, the culture will not shift. “You can talk about a lot of things, but if the person who holds that leadership role shows up in a way that contradicts all of their stated ideals, the culture is really not going to shift, and it will be much more difficult.” Role modeling, she says, is not one ingredient among many. It is the condition that determines whether everything else lands or not.

The practitioners we spoke to have each developed their own answer to the question of what cultural change actually looks like in practice, not as a set of abstract principles but as repeatable ways of being together that teams can build into their daily work.

Mette Aagaard draws heavily on the sociocracy toolbox*, a set of principles and structures she has seen work across very different organisations. “Personally, I find it hard to take part in meetings or dialogue that don’t adhere to these kinds of principles,” she says. The practices are specific and unglamorous, but that is precisely the point.

It starts with the check-in: not a 30 update on what everyone has been doing, but a brief moment to ensure that each person is genuinely present and that nothing is standing in the way of their full attention. Then there is decision-making by consent, which she is an enthusiastic advocate for because it sidesteps the power struggles that tend to emerge from consensus-based processes. Speaking in rounds ensures that talking time is distributed rather than captured by the most confident voices. Having a dedicated facilitator for each meeting means that when something difficult needs to be brought to the table, whether because the content is complex or because emotions are running high, there is someone whose explicit role is to hold the space. “It’s about helping the team refrain from moving into decision-making mode when what we are discussing is not yet ready for a decision, but needs more exploration of perspectives in order to better understand the complexity of an issue.”

Working with roles and how they are distributed and nominated among colleagues is another piece, one she acknowledges can be genuinely sensitive. And then there is transparency, which she finds teams often struggle with in unexpected ways. She describes one team that considered introducing full transparency around the registration of working hours. The idea came from a place of mutual care: without a manager surveying hours, colleagues had no way of knowing if someone was accumulating far too many and needed help or protection. But others experienced the proposal as surveillance. “I don’t want my colleagues to monitor when or how much I’m working, and I feel it becomes a competition for who’s accumulated the most hours, as in who’s the best colleague.” The conversation took a long time and carried a lot of hard feelings. Eventually, they went ahead with it. And what happened was not competition or blaming each other: they discovered that one colleague had been registering fewer hours than she should have been.When they asked what was going on, it turned out she was navigating a difficult situation at home involving a child who was struggling to attend school. “And then the team said: now that we know this, how can we help you?” The transparency that had felt threatening became the condition for support.

Chapter 3 · Challenges with distributed teams

Voices from the field

The shift from hierarchical leadership to leading together asks something from everyone involved, not just those at the top. The practitioners we spoke to have thought carefully about what it requires – both from the managers learning to let go and the employees learning to step up.

Karin Tenelius locates the starting point in something that seems simple: awareness. Most managers who operate in a parent-child dynamic with their employees don’t realise they are doing it. Neither do the employees. “It’s an invisible power, a hierarchical dynamic going on,” she says. In Sweden in particular, she observes that managers often believe they are coaching, involving and listening when, in practice, they are not. The theory is there; the behaviour has not followed. “So it’s an awakening.” And from awareness, she argues, comes the possibility of choice: the ability to notice the pattern you are in and decide, in that moment, to do something different. That choice requires honest feedback and straightforward interaction, catching yourself over and over again as old habits reassert themselves. At Tuff Leadership Training, where she and her colleagues have been self-organised for 23 years, they have developed a direct language for this. When someone slips into a passive or victim-like stance, a colleague might say: “We don’t have any employees here; we just have owners. Can you choose to be an owner? How would that sound?” It takes time, she says, especially for managers who have spent years in a different role. But it is learnable, through sustained practice and repeated choice.

Nati Lombardo approaches the same question from the manager’s perspective and offers something like a practical checklist for what genuine role modeling requires. It starts with showing up authentically: being willing to be vulnerable if you want others to be vulnerable, receiving feedback openly, and asking for it proactively rather than waiting for it to arrive. It means saying “I don’t know” when you do not know, making it visible that mistakes happen and that they are survivable, and holding yourself accountable if you want accountability to become a shared norm. “Those are probably some of the crucial pieces,” she says. Beyond that, it calls for making a real change to the way you occupy meetings and decisions: moving from directing to facilitating, from deciding to involving, from delegating tasks to opening up shared processes. “How do you involve others? How do you bring them into the conversation? How do we encourage shared decision-making or shared delegation?” For employees who are more used to a traditional passive role, Nati is honest about the challenge. Change on that side of the relationship is harder to sustain if the people with formal power have not shifted first. “You first need the leaders and the people in power to shift out of their current perspective, their roles, or their ways of doing things, to model something different, so that others can pick it up and start doing it as well. Otherwise, you can talk as much as you want.” Given that foundation, she points to a few practices that can help employees grow into more active participation. Learning facilitation skills is one, not only because it equips people to run meetings but because the act of facilitating is itself a form of stepping into leadership. “It gives you a sense of empowerment, a sense of: I can also do something that is leading the team towards some outcome.” Proactively asking for feedback is another practice she recommends, as it helps build self-awareness and opens space for the kinds of difficult conversations that most teams find hard to sustain.

One practice Nati returns to with particular enthusiasm is what she calls pods: small peer coaching groups of three to five people who meet regularly, usually every two weeks, specifically to support each other’s development. The groups are deliberately composed across different parts of the organisation, bringing together people who do not normally work together, which gradually strengthens the social fabric of the organisation. They are rotated every six months or so, so that trust and relationships keep building in new directions. What happens in a session varies by team and by moment. Sometimes someone brings a specific challenge they want to think through together. Sometimes it is more of a check-in: how are you feeling, what are you struggling with, what do you need? Some pods use more structured formats – like Troika from Liberating Structures1 or Case Clinic from Theory U2 – for delving deeper into something. Others keep it more conversational. Sometimes the focus is tied to a specific developmental goal a person is working on. “If that person is currently focused on bringing more appreciation into the team, the focus of the meetings will be on that developmental goal and how they’re doing.” Sometimes it is as immediate as: “I just need feedback on this presentation I’m preparing.”

What Nati finds significant about the pods model is what it replaces and supplements. The standard HR procedure of a once-or-twice-a-year developmental talk between a manager and an employee, with a few followup meetings scattered throughout the year, leaves people without the kind of continuous, in-time support that actually helps them grow. Peer coaching, distributed across the organisation and built into regular rhythms, begins to fill that gap. And in doing so, it shifts the culture: from one where development is something steered for you by someone above you, to one where it is something you and your colleagues are actively doing together.

Chapter 4 · The evolving role of employees: learning to take initiative and responsibility

Voice from the artistic field

In most organisations, a manager’s role comes with a very clear “score”. There are expectations about how to occupy a room, how much to speak, where to stand, and what to know. Managers are expected to have the answers, to perform and be competent, and to be the central presence in meetings. Their schedules reflect this: dense with meetings – occasions to demonstrate that they are managing others. In the workshops he facilitated, Dejan Srhoj proposed something that goes directly against all of this. What if managers became listening structures instead? What if they spent time simply hanging around the kitchen, observing with their whole body, entering meetings only when invited, attending to what was actually happening around them rather than performing their role at the front of the room?

The proposal sounds simple. What it asks of people is considerable, because the role is not just a set of behaviours. It is, as Dejan describes it, a score that people inhabit so completely that they become it. “A lot of what we do is: we perform our job role, and the role has a quite clear set of instructions. And the moment you find yourself in it, suddenly you become this role.” What dance and choreography do to make this visible is they invite people to shift perspective, to move differently in the room, and to relate to others from an unfamiliar position in which the role and the person are not the same thing. The role demands certain behaviours, but there is space, always, for negotiation between what is expected and how you actually inhabit it. We just need to provide a rehearsal ground for practising other ways of being with each other.

When that space opens up, something changes in how people are viewed and view themselves. Employees who had been silent through entire workshops began, over time, to facilitate, to propose, to lead. In one case, a person who had said almost nothing in the early sessions ended up reading a poem to a room of politicians and moving them to tears. The shift of power was not announced or planned. It emerged as people encountered themselves and each other outside the positions their roles had assigned them. “By shifting roles, or by inviting people to change perspectives, or how they see things, or how they are in the room, how they relate to others, we get different aspects and qualities of the people, and see them as individuals that have their own perks and qualities, which are different from the role that they are expected to fulfil or embody.” What becomes possible then are relations that are fuller, more trusting, more capable of genuine dialogue and even criticism, because the people in them are no longer being taken as monolithic structures but as dynamic, emerging processes. Dejan is careful about how he views managers. He does not experience them as powerful beings who could change things but choose not to. He experiences them as people under enormous and rarely acknowledged pressure, caught between the desires and needs of the people below them and the expectations and demands of the people above them, constrained from every side, and often privately longing for exactly the kind of playfulness and flexibility they are not supposed to show. “At the end of the day, you are also just a human being who also maybe wants to be playful and not give a damn, but you’re not allowed to. Or you don’t allow yourself, because you think you’re not allowed.” These restrictions he describes are real, and they are structural. What the workshops try to do, at their best, is create a temporary suspension of that structure: a space where a manager can spend a day performing as an employee, or as someone deeply critical of everything, and where an employee who has never led anything can suddenly be the one creating a choreography and discovering what it actually takes.

The invitation, ultimately, is not to blame individuals for the roles they play but to look honestly at what produces those roles. “Let’s not assume that they are simply very stiff beings afraid of change. Let’s assume that they actually want change. Let’s assume that they are not stiff, and let’s look at what does not allow them to be playful or flexible. Let’s look at the structural context to see why they are like they are.” That shift in framing, from judging people to examining the systems that shape them, is one of the quieter but more consequential things that artistic practice, with its attention to how roles are constructed and inhabited, brings to the work of leading together.

Chapter 4 · The evolving role of employees: learning to take initiative and responsibility

Voices from the field

In both Denmark and Sweden, the law requires that every employee have a person formally responsible for their workplace. That legal reality means that in the public sector, the question has never been whether to remove managers, but rather how their role needs to change. Mette Aagaard has spent years working with this question, and her answer is grounded in what she calls the hybrid model: an organisational design where formal leadership structures continue to exist alongside distributed decision-making. Here, the challenge is learning to make both work together without one undermining the other.

The clearest example she draws on is a child welfare NGO in Denmark with around 250 employees. Mette has followed them closely, and their approach, work, and impact formed an important part of the research for her own book on shared leadership*. They started their journey in 2018 and have persistently continued to develop their own model. The way they explain it is through a spatial metaphor. The traditional organisational structure, the reporting lines, the formal manager, the place where someone assesses how you are doing and whether you have access to development, is what they call “the place where you live”. It is where you feel a sense of connectedness, where someone holds responsibility for your well-being and growth. The circles, the distributed decision-making structures, they call “the place where you go to work”. The two serve different purposes and neither replaces the other.

Within that model, the role of the formal manager shifts considerably. At “home”, the manager’s job is to maintain an ongoing dialogue with each employee: asking how they are doing, if anything is standing in the way of them feeling committed and able to thrive, and whether there are conflicts that need addressing. Checking whether the base is safe. When it comes to the work, the manager’s role is to ensure that mandates are clear, that aims are well-defined, and that decision-making authority is genuinely distributed rather than just nominally so. If two teams are struggling to coordinate, the manager does not step in and solve it. Rather, the manager creates the conditions for the people involved to solve it themselves. “My role would be to invite that dialogue, but not to solve the problem. To facilitate, to keep the dialogue open, not jump into solution mode right away.” There is even a phrase for the temptation to do otherwise: “stealing decisions”. Once you have given the right to decide to someone else, you cannot come and take it back. “You have to keep them there.”

However, certain responsibilities remain with top management. Strategy, for instance, is still the responsibility of senior leaders, even if others should be involved in it. What’s important is the overall shift in the manager’s role – from the go-to fix-it person to a facilitator of dialogue, decision-making, and exploration.

According to Mette, what this shift requires goes deeper than a set of new skills. There is thinking that needs to be unlearned before new thinking can take root, and it operates at such a subconscious level that making it conscious is the necessary first step. One held assumption she often works to dismantle is the belief that consensus is the foundation of all good decision-making, and that when consensus cannot be reached, the manager must step in and decide. Replacing that assumption requires developing a genuinely different relationship to disagreement: the capacity to refrain from using whatever formal or informal power you hold to push your own preferences through, and to stay in a mode of exploration rather than resolution. “It’s perfectly fine that I don’t agree with you. I don’t need to agree with you in order for us to move forward and explore and improve and execute.” That is not primarily a skill, she says. It is a fundamental belief which, once understood and held, gradually translates into action and eventually becomes a skill.

The same logic applies to what Mette calls the dance between managers and employees, a dynamic she has seen play out in every organisation she has worked with. The steps are familiar: an employee notices something that is not working and reports it to the manager, and both parties operate from the shared assumption that it is the manager’s job to fix it. If the manager does not act, the employee is entitled to complain. And managers, she says, largely accept this arrangement, feeling the full weight of a responsibility that is both isolating and structurally impossible to discharge well. “If someone tells me two colleagues cannot collaborate, or an employee looks completely exhausted, or a department can’t make sense of a recent change, I feel it’s 100% my responsibility to fix it. That leaves managers in a very lonely place, but also a very inefficient one, because we are very rarely able to solve these kinds of issues alone.”

The dance becomes a blaming cycle. Managers want employees to take more responsibility for the shared workplace and grow frustrated when they do not. Employees feel ready to take responsibility but find that without a real mandate, their initiative leads nowhere. “It’s only if he or she agrees with me that my responsibility can actually be used for anything.” Both sides are right about the other, and neither can move without the other moving first.

The shift Mette is pointing towards calls for a replacement of that whole dynamic with something different: the idea that whatever occurs is a shared responsibility, and that simply naming a problem does not fulfil your part of it. “I offer my willingness to come up with ideas. I offer my collegial support regardless of whether you are a manager, an employee, or a colleague. We need to figure out what we can do together.” It sounds straightforward when stated plainly. She is honest: getting it to work in practice is something else entirely.

Chapter 4 · Competencies needed for managers and employees

Voices from the field

Resistance is one of the most consistent challenges in any transformation towards leading together, and it takes many forms. There are those who are sceptical from the start, those who engage initially but pull back when things get difficult, and those who have been in an organisation long enough that the prospect of changing now, perhaps close to retirement, simply does not feel worth the effort. Karin Tenelius has worked with all of these, and her approach begins with a fundamental observation: much of what appears to be resistance is usually misunderstanding.

“If people understand what it could be, most are actually positive,” she says. The problem is that in the early stages of any such process, people fill in the gaps of their understanding with their worst fears. They imagine chaos, or the loss of things they value, or something vaguely threatening that they cannot name. “There are so many misunderstandings in the beginning when introducing shared leadership that make them negative, so you have to invest a lot of time early on to distinguish what this is about, what it is and what it is not.” That investment of time upfront, she argues, is not a detour from the real work. It is the real work, because people who operate from misunderstanding will resist even the changes they would welcome if they understood them properly.

Once the misunderstandings are cleared, the next step is to move from the organisational level to the personal. It is not enough to explain why the change is good for the organisation. People need to be able to see what it means for them individually before they are genuinely ready to engage. “Getting to the level of individual self-interest is necessary before people move. If they understand what’s in it for them, they can be onboarded much more easily and quickly, without getting stuck.” And the framing matters too. Presenting change as a destination, a fixed end state that the organisation is moving towards, tends to generate more resistance than presenting it as a journey, something that will evolve and that people are part of shaping. “It’s not about ‘this is where we’re going’; it’s about starting a journey towards something, and things will evolve.”

Karin has also made a deliberate shift in the language she uses. She no longer leads with the term “self-organising”, which can trigger exactly the kind of anxious misreading she is trying to avoid. Instead, she talks about strengthening collaboration, a word that most people already believe in and want more of, and that carries none of the associations with chaos or loss of structure that self-organisation can evoke. “Real collaboration, in the end, leads to self-organising,” she says. But you do not have to start there.

Chapter 4 · Challenges with changing roles for leading together in hierarchical organisations

Voices from the field

When it comes to introducing participatory structures, Mette Aagaard’s first piece of advice is about pace: “I would definitely take it slow, because there’s so much at stake for the managers in particular.” Managers need to feel safe enough to genuinely distribute mandates, and employees need to feel confident that what is being proposed is not just another initiative that will be replaced by a different buzzword in three years. Change fatigue is real, and moving too fast feeds it.

With that caveat established, her practical recommendation is to work simultaneously across four tracks. But when asked to identify the easiest starting point, she points to something specific: find a task, not a team, that could genuinely benefit from distributed mandate and faster decision-making, and build a small experiment around that task. “Look at the task more than at the teams: which tasks could benefit from distributed or shared leadership? Then start organising a team around that task, where you would immediately start deploying the principles of shared leadership.” The key is to choose something that is meaningfully connected to daily operations, but not the most complex or high-stakes thing you could possibly attempt.

She draws on an example from an elderly care home she worked with some years ago. One team was made responsible for nutrition and food: a task that was integrated into daily operations, and where a different way of organising seemed likely to help. They formed a team around it, introduced participatory meeting structures drawing on sociocracy, defined clear roles, and developed new ways of engaging with the elderly residents living in the home. Full decision-making power was given to that team. It became, as she puts it, a small experiment that would test not only the team’s capacity to work in a new way, but the leadership team’s actual readiness to hand over the mandate.

That readiness, she says, is where the most common and most consequential pitfall lies. “It sounds so appealing to most modern leaders that they really want to do this. But then, when it comes to it, the day the team makes a decision you do not agree with, that’s usually when the mandate disappears. And that doesn’t work.” Giving a team decision-making power and then quietly retrieving it the moment a decision is made that the manager would not have made is not distributed leadership. It is distributed leadership with an invisible veto attached. “You need to figure out what to do when the team makes a decision you do not like.” Starting with a task that is relevant but not the most difficult one you can imagine is partly about manageability, and partly about giving the team a realistic chance of following through.

The same elderly care home had a second team organised around activities for residents, including sing-alongs, small excursions, visits from a neighbouring school, and knitting groups. Again, relevant and meaningful work, but not the kind of territory where a bold decision would provoke a crisis. Around that task, the organisation began structuring what distributed leadership actually required in practice: what decision-making power would look like, how roles would be distributed, what the manager’s role would become, how interactions between the team and leadership would be defined, and how targets would be set. “You have these small labs where you test the structures at a micro level,” she says, “and then afterward you start figuring out how to deploy this to a larger part of the organisation.”

The sequence matters. Starting with the whole organisational structure and trying to redesign everything at once is, in her view, simply too challenging to be workable. The small lab approach allows teams and leaders alike to learn what a distributed mandate actually feels like before they are asked to trust it at scale.

Chapter 5 · Participatory meeting structures

Voices from the field

Nati Lombardo reflects on some observations regarding decision-making processes and common tendencies around them. The pull toward consensus is understandable, being rooted in a genuine desire for inclusion, but it creates its own problems. “It falls into the trap of: everything needs to be decided by consensus, everyone needs to be involved in everything, and the pendulum swings too far the other way.” The tendency towards consensus is particularly strong in certain cultures, like Sweden. Nati’s preferred starting point is the advice process: a structure in which the person with the formal mandate to make a decision is expected to consult with those who will be affected by it or who have relevant expertise, before deciding. The final decision might still rest with the manager, but the act of genuinely seeking and listening to input changes the nature of the process in ways that matter. “Involving people in the process is what becomes important. People actually do listen.” It is a way of opening up the idea that participation is real, without the paralysis that full consensus processes can create.

For the kinds of decisions where managers might begin modelling the advice process, she thinks about situations where the outcome will directly affect the people around the decision-maker. Hiring is one example she finds useful across many different contexts: the person being brought into a team is going to work closely with colleagues who have relevant knowledge and a real stake in the outcome, so widening the process to include their perspective, even when the final decision remains with HR or a manager, makes the hiring more considered and signals something important about how the organisation operates. Budget questions are another possibility, particularly when there is discretionary money to allocate. Asking the people closest to the daily work how a pot of resources might best be used, and genuinely taking that input into account, is a concrete and low-risk way of practicing the advice process while building the habit of involving people in decisions that affect them. “In smaller teams it’s a bit easier,” she acknowledges, “but in larger organisations it can be more challenging.” The principle, however, translates across contexts. Start with decisions that are meaningful but limited in scope. Involve the people who have the most relevant knowledge and the most direct stake in the outcome. Then let the manager or person responsible for the issue make the final call, ensuring the consultation is genuinely real rather than merely procedural.

Chapter 5 · Participatory decision-making

Voice from the artistic field

One way to directly experience what participatory structures actually feel like is to build something together under a set of enabling constraints. Dejan Srhoj describes an exercise he has used in organisational workshops that makes this clear: a group has to create a three-minute performance, and each person can contribute a movement or a movement sequence. That is the score. Within it, almost everything else is open. Nobody knows in advance who will contribute what, whether there will be music, how the space will be used, if one person will end up shaping most of the choreography or whether contributions will be distributed evenly. What is known is that everyone is invited, that the knowledge and desire each person brings is a legitimate resource, and that the goal is shared.

What emerges from the exercise, in a very short time, is a lived encounter with most of the questions that shared leadership poses. How do we co-create under the pressure of a real goal? Who do we need to satisfy: ourselves, the process, an audience member (customer)? What material conditions are required, and who provides them? Leadership shifts from person to person as the work unfolds, and that shifting is not theoretical but embodied, felt in the moment of deciding to step forward or step back. “In a very short time, you get to embody many of the topics or theories of how we can share leadership. And this usually allows for a much deeper reflection on questions like: How am I acting in this group? How are we behaving together as a group? What is needed? What kind of environment do we need in order to do this collectively?”

Beyond the workshop settings, Dejan draws on examples from his own artistic practice that point towards what organisations might look like if they trusted emergence over predefinition. He describes being part of an improvisational performance called Special Edition, by choreographer Andreja Rauch Podrzavnik, in which musicians and dancers would meet and perform together for approximately an hour. The only thing decided in advance was the structure of duets, trios and quartets. Everything else, what actually happened between the performers, would emerge from the relations and the dialogue in the room. Magical moments might occur. So might complete miscommunication, egos taking over, and failures of listening and observation. “You’re interested in all of this as an audience but also as a performer. And you go consciously into that risk, and everyone involved knows what those risks are. And this is not an inhibition but actually a quality that you’re looking for.”

Dejan can imagine a team within an organisation working with a similar proposal. We have a month, we have these people, and we want to create a certain service or achieve a certain goal. But how we do it, and exactly what emerges, we do not decide in advance. We allow for experimentation, knowing that some things will produce magic and some things will fail, and that both are part of the learning process. The contrast with how most organisations actually operate is obvious: “They first define a very clear goal but also processes for how to get there, and then there’s very little space to actually experiment and find out how you could do it in different ways.”

His own practice of mapping the city offers another angle on this. Dejan sets a score, in which the audience observes everything that unfolds in a certain part of the city as if they were watching a dance performance, translating what they see into a kind of fiction. The information one can gather in this way is rich: about how people relate, about how architecture defines the choreography of movement through space, about what they themselves are attending to and what they are looking for. He brought a version of this observational practice into the organisational workshops, inviting participants to observe their own working environment as if seeing it for the first time: noticing when people arrive at the office, when they leave, when they take breaks, how they relate to each other, and whether the space invites them to connect or to be isolated. “Without the assumption that they already know. So one goes into the practice with openness to seeing what emerges.”

The most extended example he offers is a festival his collective once organised, an experiment in what a dance festival could be if it trusted its participants entirely. Instead of a fixed programme, they invited around 50 artists and performers, and asked each of them to bring along an invitation (instead of a finished artwork): an invitation to a workshop, practice, lecture, or proposal to explore something together. Each morning, the programme for the day was co-created over a shared breakfast. Nothing was scheduled in advance beyond those gathering moments. The score, as he describes it, was ten days and fifty people, with one rule: each day we create a menu for the day, and then that is it. “You trust the participants, that they have an interest in getting to know each other, sharing something, and learning together. You trust that they will show up even though they don’t need to. You understand everything that is happening as information that you can work with.”

What emerged during those ten days was not chaos but festivity in its original sense: people celebrating together, teaching things they were still learning themselves, raising the topic of care, and questioning what it means to invite artists rather than artworks – to say we want people with whatever they can bring rather than inviting products. The experiment cost them 50 percent of their budget for the following year because funders did not recognise it as a festival in the expected sense. “By pretending, you usually get more money and budgets than by actually doing what a festival is – a festivity.”

This last experience, in our opinion, illustrates the tension that runs through this handbook: between the systems that reward the appearance of control and the practices that make something new and meaningfully possible.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard doesn’t talk about where to begin, but about what kind of conviction needs to be in place before anything else. The transformations she has seen sustain themselves are those where the impulse to change is deeply tied to the organisation’s actual mission, often because an external pressure or a new strategic direction has made it impossible to continue as before. “It’s not just a communication task. It’s a fundamental conviction that we cannot continue to do what we’re doing now if we are to serve the citizens or the employees.” That conviction must be real, not rhetorical, because without genuine commitment to the underlying purpose, the journey will become cumbersome and people will quietly return to what they know.

Karin Tenelius reflects on the specific question of the public sector, and the importance of connecting the transformation towards leading together to its purpose. Around half of her clients work in the public sector, and she finds it more meaningful than private sector work. The public sector has more tolerance for inefficiency than the private sector, which also means it has more to gain. Its advantage is that it is not driven by quarterly results and tends to carry a genuine sense of purpose, of being there for citizens, for pupils, for improving the health and well-being of communities. That sense of purpose, she argues, is something many public organisations have lost touch with, and reconnecting with it is not just motivationally useful; It’s what makes transformation feel worth the cost. “Municipalities can start to look like ordinary organisations instead of finding their soul again.” And when they do reconnect with that, the scarcity of resources, which is so often experienced as a constraint, can become a driver. When you lack the resources to do everything yourself, you have every reason to activate the community around you, and that activation is itself a form of leading together with citizens.

Voices from the field

Nati Lombardo’s starting advice is directed at the change agent who does not hold formal power: find an ally who does. Without someone in a position of authority who is genuinely on board, the initiative will remain fragile. Once that foundation exists, her recommendation is to resist the temptation to go wide and instead go deep in one place. Choose a team that already has some spark for this, or a strong culture of trust, or people who are ready to experiment. Try things, see what works in that specific context and culture, and then let it spread from there organically. “You can cross-pollinate to other teams and see what takes hold.” For leaders who want to begin changing their own practice without waiting for an organisation-wide mandate, the same logic applies: bring a different way of being into the spaces you already occupy, propose something to your team, see how they respond, and build from there.

On scaling up, Nati has observed that the most sustainable expansion happens not through top-down rollout but through curiosity. When a team is working in a different way and something is visibly grabbing, useful, and enjoyable, people from other teams start to notice and want to try it themselves. “It expands organically instead of being enforced from the top.” At some point, once several groups are already experimenting, it becomes possible to establish cross-team structures that begin to hold everything together. And at the point where an organisation decides to transition more fully, a larger conversation with everyone becomes necessary: about what the change will actually entail for individuals, what it will look like in practice, and whether people really want it.

Karin Tenelius is candid about the distance between where she started and where she is now in her thinking about how to support these transitions. “I’ve gone from a very naive vision to a more down-to-earth one,” she says. She no longer encourages organisations to pursue complete transformation. She encourages them to be genuinely inspired by self-organising principles and take from them what they can realistically absorb. The conditions matter more than the ambition. Managers need to be not just intellectually persuaded but personally willing to change. If the working climate in a team is unhealthy, that needs to be addressed before new practices are introduced, because no new way of working will take root in soil that is already poisoned. And the starting point should always be the people who are already ready: the pockets of the organisation where there is genuine longing for something different. “Work with people who are engaged and brave. You can find them in any organisation. Build on those people, support them, invest in them, and see the change they can create.”

When those first teams have genuinely shifted, the most powerful thing they can do is become ambassadors, not because someone asks them to, but because they cannot help it. “Two women managers really became ambassadors because they couldn’t help themselves, they found it so valuable.” But that only happens when the results are significant enough to be undeniable. Small improvements do not generate ambassadors. Transformative experiences do. Which means doing everything possible to create the conditions for real results in the first teams before expecting the word to spread. And it’s crucial to find change agents higher up in the hierarchy, because without eventual top management engagement, the work remains–as she puts it–a guerrilla project. Fine as far as it goes, but limited in its reach.

When Nati Lombardo talks about the role of top management, she is direct: without their involvement, the change will not hold at scale. But the way they engage matters just as much as whether they engage. The worst version is a top-down announcement that the organisation is now going to selfmanage. The better version is top leadership that holds the process rather than driving the outcome– presenting the concept as a question rather than a decision. This approach models the kind of participatory stance that the transformation asks of everyone from the outset. “What would it look like if they held the process: ‘I’ve been considering this, what do you all think?’” That positioning, she argues, changes everything about how the change lands.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard insists that leaders who want to initiate this change must fully understand what it demands of them personally before they start expecting it from others. One of the most common pitfalls she sees is leaders who grasp the concept and then assume the work is now for everyone else to do. “If you want to change the organisation, everybody needs to take a critical look at their own behaviour, practice, and habits, and that goes for leaders too.” The middle management layer, she adds, is consistently underestimated and underinvested in these processes. If you want to change a hierarchy, you need the hierarchy to work with you rather than against you.

Karin Tenelius reflects on what the journey has meant for her personally. As a founder and CEO, she has had to learn to trust that her ideas can survive without her defending them, and that being vulnerable and honest about why something matters to her is more effective than using the power she has to push it through. “I’ve learned other ways that are much more collaborative.” And what she has gained is something she describes as immense freedom: the freedom of not needing to control outcomes, write reports, or monitor everything. The freedom of being surrounded by people who are honest with her, and of not needing to be afraid of honesty in return.

Nati Lombardo has spent years supporting other organisations through transitions toward self-management, but she is candid about the fact that the learning has run in both directions. When asked what she has taken from the journey personally, the first thing she names is the sheer amount of inner work involved, something she had not anticipated at the start. “I didn’t really consider that at the beginning. How much I had to look at my own behaviours and patterns.” The personal dimension of this kind of transformation, the need to examine your own habitual ways of operating and the assumptions you carry without knowing it, turns out to be as demanding as anything on the organisational side.

There are always setbacks alongside the successes, Karin Tenelius observes. Always forces working to keep things as they are, and always moments that feel like failure. But culture, she says, is like air. It can look completely stuck and then suddenly crack open. “When it looks really horrible and ugly, all of a sudden it cracks and opens. And then that is a genuine breakthrough, a true leap forward.” Which means that the most important quality for anyone doing this work is the capacity to stay connected to the process, to resist the urge to plan everything, and to stay ready for the unexpected. “You don’t have maps, really. You have a few, but very little guidance. You have to stay connected to the process all the time and see what’s happening.”

Voices from the field

Nati Lombardo suggests that retrospectives are useful for sustaining the practice of leading together over time. The ability to regularly come together and name what is or isn’t working, and continually make adjustments, allows the practice to keep evolving rather than calcifying or quietly dissolving. “At the beginning the ideal can look beautiful, but once you start putting it into practise things emerge, and it’s not so easy. So at least being able to talk about it, to name what’s not there yet, and to keep looking for improvements is key.”

Voice from the artistic field

The hardest thing about bringing an artistic practice into an organisational setting, Dejan Srhoj says, is not proposing the exercise. (That part is relatively straightforward.) The hard part is standing behind it, holding space for whatever needs to unfold, and resisting the urge to intervene. “Like a psychotherapist, my role is to hold a space for the creative process to unfold. And I feel the biggest challenge for people who haven’t done it a lot is not to propose, because that’s not so hard, but to stand behind it and to hold the space for whatever has to unfold.” It took him many years to develop that quality of presence: the ability to see very quickly what could have been done differently, what might have helped, and to leave that knowledge unused, because the experience itself is what people need, not the correction.

That is why he sees the natural next step in this work as training people both from artistic and organisational backgrounds not only to participate in co-creation processes around shared leadership, but also to facilitate and hold space for them. This knowledge should be widely distributed, not kept by a small number of practitioners.

But there is another entry point, available to anyone, that doesn’t require specific training at all. Every person already has a relationship with movement. They walk to work, breathe, sleep, go to the gym, play a sport. The question Dejan invites us to consider is, what happens when you shift something small within what you are already doing? What if the 10-minute walk from the underground to the office became a half-hour walk, with stops? What if you ran it as fast as you could? What if you took a different path? What if you walked it with a colleague in silence, or talking loudly? “We are already professionals at walking from the underground to the office, we are already professionals at observing the space. What if I just observed it for a longer or shorter time?” These are not trivial variations. They are scores, in the choreographic sense: simple enabling constraints that change how you inhabit time, space and relationships that generate real information about yourself and others.

The same logic applies inside the workplace. What if the meeting rooms were rearranged? What if the meetings were held in a forest, or conducted while moving? What if there were more time, or less, for a meeting? He describes a score developed by choreographer Lisa Nelson in the 1970s, in which a group closes their eyes and simultaneously creates a movement. What tends to happen is that people begin making the same movement, or nearly the same movement, without being able to see each other. Something in the shared space has been informed in a particular way, and people begin working from it without knowing how. “I don’t know how it’s possible. But there’s something that the space has been informed with in a certain way, and people suddenly start to work from that common space.”

For more extended or demanding processes, facilitation and training are necessary. But for beginning, for shifting something small in everyday life and seeing what it opens, the knowledge required is already in the room.

For those who want to go further and collaborate with artists directly, Dejan’s first piece of advice is to address the prejudices that run in both directions. Artists often carry an assumption that organisations are purely efficiency and profitdriven, indifferent to everything that does not generate output. Organisations often assume that artists are unreliable, working from mysterious inspiration rather than a specific method and knowledge. All these assumptions get in the way of what could be a productive collaboration. “Artists are workers. They have methods, they have a lot of knowledge, they have a lot of references and experiences.” The first step is personal: finding an artist whose work interests you, approaching them, and starting a dialogue. It takes time, and it is not self-evident that it will work. But there must be a basic trust that there is knowledge on both sides worth sharing.

Dejan is also clear that not all art is the same, just as not all organisations are the same. Within choreography, as within any field, there are practitioners who work in deeply traditional, hierarchical ways and have no interest in questioning authority or structure. What matters is finding the right meeting point between a specific artistic sensibility and a specific organisational readiness. “I would not say there is one recipe. There is really a dialogue.”

What he’s observed in the organisations that have engaged with this work is that change tends to begin in small and concrete ways. Managers who participated in the workshops reported learning how important it is to create space at the start of a meeting for people to arrive, slow down, and land in their bodies before the work begins. Some have started reading a poem or conducting a short breathing or grounding exercise with participants. Some have begun rethinking the spaces where they meet, taking their team into a forest instead of a meeting room. Some have shifted their own roles, moving towards the listening structure proposed in the workshops proposed and finding ways to be present and attentive rather than performing and directing. One manager described wanting to give their team time to simply sit in the kitchen for a few hours, observing and listening. “Things are starting to happen,” Dejan says. “But you have to be quite courageous to question these existing patterns in organisations with a lot of established expectations.” The courage required is not dramatic. It is the quieter courage of doing something differently, in a context where the pressure to do things as they have always been done is constant and palpable.

Voices from the field

Mette Aagaard is firm that this process must be understood as an organisational transformation, not a toolbox. “Organisations that approach this as if it’s a toolbox, you take a course, learn to use the tools, start implementing, and then when that doesn’t solve all the problems, look for another toolbox, that’s a different thing.” If you genuinely think of it as a transformation, approach it that way.

One concrete investment Mette always recommends in the startup phase is developing facilitation skills. Not everyone needs to be trained, but the people who will facilitate dialogues do, and so do the leaders who will be giving them the mandate to do so. Once the process is underway, she recommends having some kind of unit or entity–ideally itself organised through distributed leadership–whose ongoing role is to sense what the organisation needs and respond: developing new training, facilitating new dialogues, creating new tools as the situation demands. And on the question of how far along any organisation is, the most honest answer she has heard comes from organisations that say they do not know, because they are never done. “It’s a constant development journey. You have to love the journey. It’s not a matter of getting there.”

Karin Tenelius has advice for the change agents who feel alone in this work–which, in her experience, is most of them. “Form communities across organisations. Create communities where you can find your allies, and gain energy and inspiration from them.” And for those encountering colleagues who are skeptical or resistant, her counsel is to not argue or persuade, but to listen to them, understand what their concerns actually are, let them be where they are, and trust that they will find their way when the time is right. “Respect them, listen to them, and be genuinely interested in what’s bothering them. That’s the way.”

Nati Lombardo adds another piece of advice. This work is complex. It is not a switch you flick. It requires personal involvement and ongoing inner work alongside the organisational changes. But it is also a field with a growing body of collective experience to draw on, and nobody starting out today needs to begin from nothing. “A lot of people are out there trying it. You don’t have to reinvent the wheel. You can build on what all of us have tried in the past. You’re not alone.”
